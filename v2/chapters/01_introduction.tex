\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

A machine learning model trained only on chest X-rays is shown a brain MRI. Nothing
in its training prepared it for a different imaging modality, yet it does not
hesitate: it returns a confident class label, as though the scan were one more X-ray.
A large language model is asked for the source of a quotation it has never seen, and
it supplies one---an author, a year, a journal---fluent, specific, and entirely
invented. Both systems fail in the same revealing way. Each has met an input, or
produced an output, that lies outside what it can be trusted to handle, and neither
can tell. The capability that is missing in both cases is not accuracy but
\emph{self-knowledge}: the ability to recognize the boundary of one's own
competence.

This dissertation is about that capability and the conditions under which a model
can possess it. Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection---recognizing inputs unlike the
training data~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline,huang2020survey}---and hallucination
detection---recognizing unsupported generated content~\citep{li2023halueval,lin2021truthfulqa}---are
usually studied as separate problems, with separate benchmarks and separate methods.
We argue that they are two faces of one question, and that one condition governs
both. \textbf{The thesis defended in this dissertation is that detection is possible
exactly when the learned representation preserves the structure that distinguishes
the shift.} Where that structure is absent from the representation, no scoring rule
can recover it, and detection is impossible. Where training has suppressed structure
that the data contained, detection degrades---but the structure can be restored.
Where the representation retains the structure, a principled detector can read it
out. Reliability, on this view, is a property of what a representation preserves.

We examine that single condition with two complementary instruments. Information
theory measures what a representation preserves as \emph{mutual information}---how
much the features still tell us about the distinction we care
about~\citep{shannon1948mathematical,cover1999elements}. Representation geometry
measures the same preservation as \emph{structure in feature space}---which
directions carry variance, which subspaces have collapsed. These are not two
subjects but two lenses on one quantity, and the dissertation deploys them across a
single arc---\emph{failure, recovery, detection}---traced by three studies and
summarized in \Cref{fig:intro-spine}. The remainder of this chapter states the
problem each study addresses, enumerates the contributions, explains the
representation-structure methodology that unifies them, and maps the document.

\section{Problem Statement and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

The reliability of a deployed model rests on its ability to recognize when an input,
or an output, falls outside what it was trained to handle. Supervised learning
optimizes prediction within the training distribution; it does not, by itself,
endow a model with awareness of that distribution's
boundary~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline}. In the open world, where the closed-set
assumption is routinely violated, this gap is not a corner case but the central
obstacle to safe deployment---the X-ray model and the fabricating language model of
the opening are not exotic failures but the ordinary behavior of confident systems
pushed past their training experience.

This dissertation addresses the gap in three settings that, together, span the
condition at the heart of the thesis. Each asks the same question---does the
representation preserve what distinguishes the shift?---and each receives a
different answer, because the structure is available to a different degree.

\paragraph{Unlabeled OOD detection: structure absent.}
A growing body of work seeks to detect distributional shift without labels, using
self- or unsupervised objectives that are attractive precisely because they need no
annotation. The difficulty is that when the features such an objective learns are
independent of the label-relevant distinction, the representation contains no
information about that distinction, and detection against it cannot succeed---a
failure mode we term \emph{label blindness}. The problem is most acute exactly where
safety demands the most: when OOD inputs share features with in-distribution data,
so that the very overlap a deployed system will encounter is the regime in which the
unlabeled detector is blind.

\paragraph{Single-domain OOD detection: structure suppressed.}
Many real systems are trained on a single narrow domain---one imaging modality, one
sensor, one institution's data. Supervised training in this setting drives the
representation into a low-rank, class-aligned subspace and suppresses the directions
along which a domain shift would register. The information needed to detect the
shift was present in the data, but training discarded it, and distance- and
logit-based detectors consequently lose sensitivity to the shifts they are meant to
catch. Unlike label blindness this is not a fundamental impossibility; it is an
\emph{induced} degradation, and what training removes, training can be made to
restore.

\paragraph{Hallucination in large language models: structure retained.}
Large language models generate fluent text that is often unsupported by
fact~\citep{li2023halueval,lin2021truthfulqa}. Existing detectors lean on repeated
sampling or external verification, and the field has lacked a principled account of
\emph{why} the signal of a hallucination should be present at all, and \emph{where}.
We show that the relevant structure is retained in the model's own intermediate
activations, and that an information-theoretic argument predicts it can be read out
directly---turning hallucination detection from an engineering search into a
consequence of how information flows through the network.

\section{Research Objectives and Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This dissertation makes the following contributions, each developed in a dedicated
chapter and each an instance of the unifying condition stated above. We summarize
them here and discharge the cross-cutting synthesis in \Cref{ch:conclusion}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A guaranteed-failure result for unlabeled OOD detection
  (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}).} We prove that when the self- or unsupervised
  surrogate objective is independent of the label-relevant features, the learned
  minimal sufficient statistic carries no information about the label distinction
  and OOD detection is \emph{guaranteed to fail}---the dissertation's one genuine
  impossibility result. We show the triggering conditions are unavoidable in
  realistic deployments, and we introduce the \emph{Adjacent OOD} benchmark, which
  constructs the feature-overlap regime that exposes the failure and that standard
  near/far benchmarks systematically avoid. Proofs appear in \Cref{app:proofs}.

  \item \textbf{A diagnosis and a recovery for single-domain OOD detection
  (\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}).} We identify and formalize
  \emph{domain-sensitivity collapse} (DSC)---the geometric mechanism by which
  single-domain supervised training suppresses domain-shift directions---and give
  \emph{diagnostic} bounds (not impossibility guarantees) that predict where
  distance- and logit-based scorers degrade. We then introduce \emph{Teacher-Guided
  Training} (TGT), a training-time auxiliary objective that distills a frozen
  multi-domain teacher to restore domain-sensitive geometry with no inference-time
  overhead. Proofs appear in \Cref{app:dsc-proofs}.

  \item \textbf{An information-theoretic hallucination detector
  (\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}).} We give a feasibility-by-trainability
  argument---cross-layer mutual information in the residual stream is positive by
  construction, so a contrastive objective that descends below its information floor
  is itself evidence the signal exists---and build a one-class contrastive probe
  over cross-layer activation pairs on that principle. The detector
  \emph{matches-or-outperforms, in the mean,} the strongest engineered probe,
  reaching \emph{parity} that is itself evidence the principle is correct rather
  than a leaderboard claim; a falsifiable prediction of the theory (the failure of
  symmetric supervision) is confirmed by ablation. Proofs appear in
  \Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs}.

  \item \textbf{A unifying representation-structure framework.} Across the three
  studies we develop and demonstrate a single methodological lens: reliability is
  governed by what the representation preserves, and information theory and geometry
  are interchangeable instruments for measuring it. This framework, not any one of
  the three results in isolation, is the dissertation's central contribution, and
  \Cref{ch:conclusion} draws the three together under it.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology and Approach}
\label{sec:intro:methodology}

The methodological commitment that unifies this work is to ask, of each reliability
problem, a single question: \emph{what does the learned representation preserve?}
Reliability is treated not as a property of a scoring rule bolted onto a trained
model, but as a property of the representation the model has
learned---specifically, whether that representation still carries the structure that
distinguishes the shift to be detected. This reframing is what lets three
superficially unrelated problems be addressed as one.

We measure preserved structure through two lenses, used interchangeably as each
problem makes one or the other sharper. The first is information-theoretic. The
distinguishing signal is mutual information between the representation and the
label-relevant distinction, and the data-processing inequality makes precise when
that information can or cannot survive a learned
map~\citep{shannon1948mathematical,cover1999elements,tishby2000information}; this
lens carries the impossibility result of \Cref{ch:label-blindness} and the
feasibility argument of \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}. The second is geometric.
The same preserved structure appears as variance along particular directions and as
the rank and orientation of class versus domain-shift subspaces, and its loss
appears as collapse; this lens carries the diagnosis and recovery of
\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}. The two are duals, not rivals: the
information a representation discards and the geometric directions it flattens are
two descriptions of one event. \Cref{ch:background} develops the primitives of both
lenses, which the results chapters then reuse.

The work combines theory with empirical validation, and holds to an honesty
discipline throughout. Impossibility is claimed only where it is proved
(\Cref{ch:label-blindness}); the bounds of \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} are
presented as diagnostic, not as guarantees; and the detector of
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} is reported as reaching parity with the strongest
baseline, with its losses shown rather than hidden. The claim of this dissertation
is that it maps the frontier of detectability and shows that principled methods
reach it---not that those methods are optimal.

\section{Dissertation Organization}
\label{sec:intro:organization}

The remainder of the dissertation is organized as follows.
\Cref{ch:background} develops the lens-neutral primitives that the results chapters
reuse, presenting both the information-theoretic and the geometric vocabulary the
title promises. \Cref{ch:literature-review} positions the three studies against
prior work, organized around the same two lenses so that the review reads as one
argument rather than three.

The three results chapters then trace the arc of \Cref{fig:intro-spine}.
\Cref{ch:label-blindness} proves the label blindness theorem and introduces the
Adjacent OOD benchmark, establishing the regime in which unlabeled detection cannot
succeed. \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} diagnoses domain-sensitivity collapse
in single-domain models and presents Teacher-Guided Training as a recovery.
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} derives and validates the information-theoretic
hallucination detector. \Cref{ch:conclusion} draws the three together under the
representation-structure framework, states the dissertation's limitations, and sets
out future directions. Supporting proofs appear in three appendices, paralleling the
three results chapters: \Cref{app:proofs} (label blindness),
\Cref{app:dsc-proofs} (domain-sensitivity collapse), and
\Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs} (hallucination detection).

\section{Significance}
\label{sec:intro:significance}

The significance of this work is both foundational and practical. Foundationally, it
argues that a single condition---the preservation of distinguishing structure---underlies
reliability across tasks as different as unlabeled OOD detection, single-domain OOD
detection, and language model hallucination, and that information theory and geometry
are two languages for one underlying account. Knowing \emph{when} a class of methods
can work at all, before measuring how well a particular instance performs, is a
contribution that empirical benchmarking alone cannot supply: a guaranteed-failure
result tells a practitioner what not to trust, and a feasibility argument tells one
where a signal must reside.

Practically, each study yields guidance for deployment. The label blindness result
and the Adjacent OOD benchmark caution against unlabeled detection in the
feature-overlap regimes that safety-critical systems actually face. The diagnosis of
domain-sensitivity collapse, and its no-overhead remedy, speak directly to the narrow
-domain models common in medical imaging, remote sensing, and industrial inspection.
The hallucination detector offers a single-pass route to flagging unsupported
generation where factual accuracy matters. More broadly, the dissertation
contributes to AI safety a posture that complements empirical evaluation with
principled accounts of the conditions under which reliability is achievable at
all---a posture \Cref{ch:conclusion} returns to once the evidence is in.
