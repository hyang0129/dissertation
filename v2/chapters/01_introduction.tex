\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

A machine learning model trained only on chest X-rays is shown a brain MRI. Nothing
in its training prepared it for a different imaging modality, yet it does not
hesitate: it returns a confident class label, as though the scan were one more X-ray.
A large language model is asked for the source of a quotation it has never seen, and
it supplies one anyway: an author, a year, a journal, all fluent, specific, and
entirely invented. Both systems fail in the same way. Each has met an input, or
produced an output, that lies outside its competence, and neither can tell. Both
cases lack not accuracy but \emph{self-knowledge}: the ability to recognize the
boundary of one's own competence.

This dissertation studies that capability through two detection problems.
Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection flags an input unlike the training
data~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline,huang2020survey}, and hallucination detection
flags a generation not supported by
fact~\citep{li2023halueval,lin2021truthfulqa}. The literature treats them as
separate problems with separate machinery, but both turn on the same thing.
\textbf{A shift is detectable exactly when the model's learned representation still
encodes the distinguishing structure.}

We read the condition through two lenses. Information theory casts what a
representation holds as \emph{mutual information}, the amount its features still
encode about the relevant
distinction~\citep{shannon1948mathematical,cover1999elements}. Representation geometry
casts the same thing as \emph{structure in feature space}: which directions carry
variance, and which have collapsed. The two name one quantity. Three studies put the
condition to work along one arc, from failure to recovery to detection
(\Cref{fig:intro-spine}).

\section{Problem Statement and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

A deployed model is trustworthy only when it detects inputs and outputs outside its
training territory. Supervised learning tunes prediction inside the training
distribution and tells a model nothing about where that distribution
stops~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline}. Deployment in the open world breaks the
closed-set assumption; that gap blocks safe deployment. The X-ray model and the
fabricating language model from the opening are not exotic failures. They are what a
confident model does once it is pushed past its training experience.

We take up this gap in three settings. Each asks whether the representation still
encodes the relevant structure, and each answers differently because the structure
survives to a different degree.

\paragraph{Unlabeled OOD detection: structure absent.}
Unlabeled methods train on self- or unsupervised objectives; when those objectives
ignore label-relevant features, the representation encodes no label distinction and
detection fails. We name this failure mode \emph{label blindness}. Label blindness
bites hardest where safety is at stake: when OOD inputs overlap in-distribution data
in feature space, unlabeled detection fails exactly where deployment demands it.

\paragraph{Single-domain OOD detection: structure suppressed.}
Many real systems train on a single narrow domain, one imaging modality or one
sensor or one institution's records. Here supervised training compresses the
representation into a class-aligned, low-rank subspace and erases the domain-shift
directions. Training contained the signal; the optimizer discarded it. Distance- and
logit-based detectors are then blind to the very shifts they should catch---not the
fundamental impossibility of label blindness. Training induces the failure, and
retraining can restore the lost directions.

\paragraph{Hallucination in large language models: structure retained.}
Large language models routinely produce fluent text with no basis in
fact~\citep{li2023halueval,lin2021truthfulqa}. Most detectors rely on repeated
sampling or outside verification; the field has no principled account of why a
hallucination signal exists, let alone where it lives. The signal sits in the
model's own intermediate activations; an information-theoretic argument guarantees it
can be extracted directly. Hallucination detection requires reading the
representation, not an engineering trick.

\section{Research Objectives and Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This dissertation contributes four results. \Cref{ch:conclusion} draws them together.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A guaranteed-failure result for unlabeled OOD detection
  (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}).} We prove that an unlabeled detector cannot work in
  the worst case once its self- or unsupervised objective is independent of the
  label-relevant features: the representation retains no label-distinction structure,
  so OOD detection is \emph{guaranteed to fail}. This is the dissertation's one
  genuine impossibility result. Its triggering conditions arise in ordinary
  deployments, and we build the \emph{Adjacent OOD} benchmark, which constructs
  high feature-overlap settings---the regime that standard near/far suites sidestep.
  Proofs appear in \Cref{app:proofs}.

  \item \textbf{A diagnosis and a recovery for single-domain OOD detection
  (\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}).} We identify and formalize
  \emph{domain-sensitivity collapse} (DSC): training a supervised model on one domain
  flattens the domain-shift directions. Geometric bounds predict where distance- and
  logit-based scorers go blind, and those bounds are \emph{diagnostic}, not
  impossibility guarantees. We then introduce \emph{Teacher-Guided Training} (TGT),
  a training-time auxiliary objective that distills a frozen multi-domain teacher,
  restoring the lost geometry at no inference cost. Proofs appear in
  \Cref{app:dsc-proofs}.

  \item \textbf{An information-theoretic hallucination detector
  (\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}).} We give a feasibility-by-trainability
  argument. Cross-layer mutual information in the residual stream is positive by
  construction, so a contrastive objective can drop below its information floor only
  when a hallucination signal is present. We build a one-class contrastive probe over
  pairs of cross-layer activations. The detector \emph{matches-or-outperforms, in the
  mean,} the strongest engineered probe. Parity is what the theory predicts. The
  theory also makes a falsifiable prediction, that symmetric supervision fails, and an
  ablation bears it out. Proofs appear in \Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs}.

  \item \textbf{A unifying representation-structure framework.} One idea governs all
  three studies: reliability depends on the preserved structure, and information
  theory and geometry are two ways to measure it. This framework, not any one result,
  is the dissertation's main contribution.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology and Approach}
\label{sec:intro:methodology}

The method is to put one question to every problem: \emph{what does the learned
representation preserve?} We treat reliability as a property of the representation
itself, not of a score bolted onto a finished model. The representation must encode
the distinguishing structure. That one question unifies three problems that would
otherwise look unrelated.

We gauge the preserved structure in two ways and take whichever is clearer. The first
is information-theoretic: mutual information between the representation and the
label-relevant features measures the signal, and the data-processing inequality
governs whether that information survives the learned
map~\citep{shannon1948mathematical,cover1999elements,tishby2000information}. This view
grounds the impossibility result of \Cref{ch:label-blindness} and the feasibility
argument of \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}. The second is geometric: the same
structure appears as variance along particular directions, and as the rank and
orientation of the class and shift subspaces, and its loss appears as collapse. This
view carries the diagnosis and recovery of \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}. The
two views land on one thing, since discarded information and collapsed directions are
the same thing. \Cref{ch:background} defines the primitives both need.

Every study pairs theory with experiment and respects its own reach. We assert
impossibility only where we prove it (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}). We give the bounds
of \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} as diagnostic, not as guarantees. We report
the detector of \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} as reaching parity with the
strongest baseline, losses shown and not buried. The dissertation identifies the
conditions under which detection works and demonstrates principled methods at that
boundary.

\section{Dissertation Organization}
\label{sec:intro:organization}

\Cref{ch:background} defines the lens-neutral primitives the results chapters
share---the information-theoretic and the geometric vocabulary the title promises.
\Cref{ch:literature-review} positions the three studies against prior work through
the same two lenses, so the review forms one argument.

\Cref{ch:label-blindness} proves the label blindness theorem and introduces the
Adjacent OOD benchmark, pinning down the regime where unlabeled detection cannot
work. \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} diagnoses domain-sensitivity collapse in
single-domain models and offers Teacher-Guided Training as the recovery.
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} derives the information-theoretic hallucination
detector and tests it. \Cref{ch:conclusion} unifies the three studies, states the
limitations, and identifies the open directions. Supporting proofs sit in three
appendices, one per results chapter: \Cref{app:proofs} (label blindness),
\Cref{app:dsc-proofs} (domain-sensitivity collapse), and
\Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs} (hallucination detection).

\section{Significance}
\label{sec:intro:significance}

This work contributes on two levels, foundational and practical. One condition
governs reliability from OOD image detection to hallucination catching; information
theory and geometry are two readings of it. A benchmark scores only the methods it
runs; it cannot determine whether a whole class of methods can work. The label
blindness theorem tells a practitioner which regimes to avoid; the feasibility
argument identifies where the signal must exist.

Each study yields concrete guidance for deployment. The label blindness result and
the Adjacent OOD benchmark warn against unlabeled detection in the feature-overlap
regimes safety-critical systems actually meet. The domain-sensitivity collapse
diagnosis, and its no-overhead fix, bear directly on the narrow-domain models common
in medical imaging and remote sensing. The hallucination detector flags unsupported
generation in a single pass, wherever factual accuracy matters. Read together, the
three studies give AI safety a principled account of when reliability is
achievable---a complement to benchmarking.
