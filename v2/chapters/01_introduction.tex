\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

A machine learning model trained only on chest X-rays is shown a brain MRI. Nothing
in its training prepared it for a different imaging modality, yet it does not
hesitate: it returns a confident class label, as though the scan were one more X-ray.
A large language model is asked for the source of a quotation it has never seen, and
it supplies one anyway: an author, a year, a journal, all fluent, specific, and
entirely invented. Both systems fail in the same way. Each has met an input, or
produced an output, that lies outside what it can be trusted to handle, and neither
can tell. What is missing in both cases is not accuracy but \emph{self-knowledge}:
the ability to recognize the boundary of one's own competence.

This dissertation studies that capability. It looks at two detection problems.
Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection recognizes inputs unlike the training
data~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline,huang2020survey}. Hallucination detection
recognizes generated content that is not supported by
fact~\citep{li2023halueval,lin2021truthfulqa}. The two are usually treated as
separate problems with separate methods, but we argue that one condition governs
both. \textbf{Detection is possible exactly when the learned representation
preserves the structure that distinguishes the shift.} That structure can be
missing, weakened, or present, and each case gives a different result. When the
representation does not contain it, no scoring rule can recover it, and detection
fails. When training has discarded structure that the data contained, detection
degrades, but the structure can be restored. When the representation keeps it, a
detector can read it out.

We study this condition with two tools. Information theory measures what a
representation preserves as \emph{mutual information}, the amount the features still
tell us about the distinction we care
about~\citep{shannon1948mathematical,cover1999elements}. Representation geometry
measures the same thing as \emph{structure in feature space}: which directions carry
variance, and which have collapsed. The two tools describe one quantity, and we use
whichever is clearer for a given problem. Three studies apply them in turn, and the
studies follow one arc: failure, recovery, detection (\Cref{fig:intro-spine}). The
rest of this chapter states the problem each study addresses, lists the
contributions, explains the method that unifies them, and maps the document.

\section{Problem Statement and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

A deployed model is reliable only if it can recognize when an input, or an output,
falls outside what it was trained to handle. Supervised learning optimizes
prediction within the training distribution. It does not, on its own, teach a model
where that distribution ends~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline}. In the open world the
closed-set assumption is routinely violated, so this gap becomes the central
obstacle to safe deployment. The X-ray model and the fabricating language model from
the opening are not exotic failures. They are the ordinary behavior of a confident
model pushed past its training experience.

We study this gap in three settings. Each setting asks whether the representation
preserves what distinguishes the shift, and each gives a different answer, because
the structure is available to a different degree.

\paragraph{Unlabeled OOD detection: structure absent.}
Recent work tries to detect distributional shift without labels, using self- or
unsupervised objectives that need no annotation. The problem is that when the
features such an objective learns are independent of the label-relevant features,
the representation carries no information about the labels, and detection cannot
succeed. We call this failure mode \emph{label blindness}. The failure is worst
where safety matters most. When OOD inputs share features with in-distribution data,
the unlabeled detector is blind in exactly the regime a deployed system will meet.

\paragraph{Single-domain OOD detection: structure suppressed.}
Many real systems are trained on a single narrow domain: one imaging modality, one
sensor, one institution's data. In this setting, supervised training pushes the
representation into a low-rank, class-aligned subspace and suppresses the directions
along which a domain shift would show up. The information needed to detect the shift
was in the data, but training discarded it, so distance- and logit-based detectors
lose sensitivity to the shifts they should catch. Unlike label blindness, this is
not a fundamental impossibility. It is an \emph{induced} failure, and training can
be changed to restore what it removed.

\paragraph{Hallucination in large language models: structure retained.}
Large language models often generate fluent text that is not supported by
fact~\citep{li2023halueval,lin2021truthfulqa}. Most detectors rely on repeated
sampling or external verification, and the field has had no principled account of
why a hallucination signal should be present, or where it lives. We show that the
signal is retained in the model's own intermediate activations, and that an
information-theoretic argument says it can be read out directly. Detecting a
hallucination then becomes a matter of reading the representation rather than
searching for an engineering trick.

\section{Research Objectives and Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This dissertation makes four contributions, each developed in its own chapter and
each an instance of the condition above. \Cref{ch:conclusion} draws them together.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A guaranteed-failure result for unlabeled OOD detection
  (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}).} We prove that when the self- or unsupervised
  objective is independent of the label-relevant features, the features the model
  learns carry no information about the label distinction and OOD detection is
  \emph{guaranteed to fail}. This is the dissertation's one genuine impossibility
  result. We show that the triggering conditions are unavoidable in realistic
  deployments, and we introduce the \emph{Adjacent OOD} benchmark, which builds the
  feature-overlap regime that exposes the failure and that standard near/far
  benchmarks avoid. Proofs appear in \Cref{app:proofs}.

  \item \textbf{A diagnosis and a recovery for single-domain OOD detection
  (\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}).} We identify and formalize
  \emph{domain-sensitivity collapse} (DSC), the geometric mechanism by which
  single-domain supervised training suppresses domain-shift directions, and we give
  \emph{diagnostic} bounds (not impossibility guarantees) that predict where
  distance- and logit-based scorers degrade. We then introduce \emph{Teacher-Guided
  Training} (TGT), a training-time auxiliary objective that distills a frozen
  multi-domain teacher to restore domain-sensitive geometry with no inference-time
  overhead. Proofs appear in \Cref{app:dsc-proofs}.

  \item \textbf{An information-theoretic hallucination detector
  (\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}).} We give a feasibility-by-trainability
  argument: cross-layer mutual information in the residual stream is positive by
  construction, so a contrastive objective that descends below its information floor
  is evidence the signal exists. On that principle we build a one-class contrastive
  probe over cross-layer activation pairs. The detector
  \emph{matches-or-outperforms, in the mean,} the strongest engineered probe.
  Reaching parity with a heavily engineered method supports the principle rather
  than topping a leaderboard, and a falsifiable prediction of the theory (the
  failure of symmetric supervision) is confirmed by ablation. Proofs appear in
  \Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs}.

  \item \textbf{A unifying representation-structure framework.} The three studies
  share one idea: reliability depends on what the representation preserves, and
  information theory and geometry both measure it. This framework, not any single
  result, is the dissertation's main contribution.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology and Approach}
\label{sec:intro:methodology}

Our approach is to ask one question of each problem: \emph{what does the learned
representation preserve?} We treat reliability as a property of the representation
itself, not of a scoring rule attached to a finished model. What matters is whether
the representation still carries the structure that distinguishes the shift. Asking
this one question is what lets us treat three unrelated-looking problems as one.

We measure preserved structure two ways and use whichever is clearer. The first is
information-theoretic. The signal is the mutual information between the
representation and the label-relevant features, and the data-processing inequality
says when that information can survive a learned
map~\citep{shannon1948mathematical,cover1999elements,tishby2000information}. This
view carries the impossibility result of \Cref{ch:label-blindness} and the
feasibility argument of \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}. The second is geometric.
The same structure shows up as variance along particular directions and as the rank
and orientation of the class and domain-shift subspaces, and its loss shows up as
collapse. This view carries the diagnosis and recovery of
\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}. The two describe the same thing: the
information a representation discards is also the set of directions it flattens.
\Cref{ch:background} defines the primitives both views need.

Each study combines theory with experiments, and each is careful about what it
claims. We claim impossibility only where we prove it (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}).
We present the bounds in \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} as diagnostic, not as
guarantees. We report the detector in \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} as reaching
parity with the strongest baseline, and we show its losses rather than hide them.
The dissertation shows when detection is possible and that principled methods reach
that limit. It does not claim they are the best possible.

\section{Dissertation Organization}
\label{sec:intro:organization}

The rest of the dissertation is organized as follows. \Cref{ch:background} defines
the lens-neutral primitives that the results chapters reuse, covering both the
information-theoretic and the geometric vocabulary the title promises.
\Cref{ch:literature-review} places the three studies against prior work, organized
around the same two lenses so that the review reads as one argument rather than
three.

The three results chapters then trace the arc of \Cref{fig:intro-spine}.
\Cref{ch:label-blindness} proves the label blindness theorem and introduces the
Adjacent OOD benchmark, establishing the regime in which unlabeled detection cannot
succeed. \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} diagnoses domain-sensitivity collapse
in single-domain models and presents Teacher-Guided Training as a recovery.
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} derives and validates the information-theoretic
hallucination detector. \Cref{ch:conclusion} draws the three together under the
representation-structure framework, states the dissertation's limitations, and sets
out future directions. Supporting proofs appear in three appendices, one per results
chapter: \Cref{app:proofs} (label blindness), \Cref{app:dsc-proofs}
(domain-sensitivity collapse), and \Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs} (hallucination
detection).

\section{Significance}
\label{sec:intro:significance}

This work matters in two ways, one foundational and one practical. The foundational
point is that a single condition, whether the representation preserves the
distinguishing structure, underlies reliability across problems as different as
unlabeled OOD detection, single-domain OOD detection, and language model
hallucination, and that information theory and geometry are two descriptions of that
one condition. Knowing \emph{when} a class of methods can work, before measuring how
well one method performs, is something benchmarks alone cannot tell you. A
guaranteed-failure result tells a practitioner what not to trust. A feasibility
argument tells them where a signal must be.

The practical point is that each study gives concrete guidance for deployment. The
label blindness result and the Adjacent OOD benchmark caution against unlabeled
detection in the feature-overlap regimes that safety-critical systems actually face.
The diagnosis of domain-sensitivity collapse, and its no-overhead remedy, speak
directly to the narrow-domain models common in medical imaging, remote sensing, and
industrial inspection. The hallucination detector offers a single-pass way to flag
unsupported generation where factual accuracy matters. More broadly, the work adds
to AI safety a habit that complements benchmarking: a principled account of when
reliability is achievable at all. \Cref{ch:conclusion} returns to this point once
the evidence is in.
