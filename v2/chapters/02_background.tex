\chapter{Background and Definitions}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter fixes the shared vocabulary the rest of the dissertation reuses.
It stays inside two boundaries. First, it holds only \emph{primitives}, the notions
used by more than one later chapter. Each named phenomenon or method is defined
where it is contributed, so the Label-Blindness theorem
(\Cref{ch:label-blindness}), Domain-Sensitivity Collapse and Teacher-Guided
Training (\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}), and the one-class layer-pair
probe (\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}) are developed in their own chapters, not
here. Second, this chapter \emph{defines} each primitive, while
\Cref{ch:literature-review} surveys how the field has used it. Background defines,
the literature review situates, and the results chapters deploy. The chapter follows
that plan. \Cref{sec:bg:ood} through \Cref{sec:bg:llm} fix the settings and tasks,
and \Cref{sec:bg:infotheory,sec:bg:ib,sec:bg:geometry} introduce the two lenses,
information theory and representation geometry, in the order
\Cref{ch:literature-review} takes them up. Citations here are limited to
foundational attributions; the surrounding literature is deferred to
\Cref{ch:literature-review}.

\section{Out-of-Distribution Detection}
\label{sec:bg:ood}

Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection is the task of recognizing when an input falls
outside the scope of a model's training distribution. Supervised models predict
well within the distribution of their training data but are prone to confident,
wrong predictions on novel inputs. Such failures have serious consequences in
high-stakes settings such as healthcare, autonomous driving, and security. The
task targets a \emph{semantic shift}~\citep{yang2021generalized}: the case in which
no in-distribution label can match the true label, $\rvy \notin \sY_{\text{in}}$,
where $\sY_{\text{in}}$ is the set of in-distribution training labels. Equivalently,
a detector estimates the probability $P(\rvy \notin \sY_{\text{in}} \mid \rvx)$ that
a sample lies outside the known semantic space.

\begin{definition}[Out-of-Distribution Detection]
Given an input $\rvx$ and a set of in-distribution labels $\sY_{\text{in}}$, OOD
detection is the task of identifying whether the true label $\rvy$ lies outside the
in-distribution set, $\rvy \notin \sY_{\text{in}}$, equivalently estimating
$P(\rvy \notin \sY_{\text{in}} \mid \rvx)$.
\label{def:bg:ood}
\end{definition}

The canonical baseline reads a confidence signal directly off a trained
classifier's output distribution.

\begin{method}[Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP)]
Given an input $\rvx$, the MSP method~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline} estimates the
probability of being out-of-distribution as $1 - \mathrm{MSP}(\rvx)$, where
$\mathrm{MSP}(\rvx) = \max_{y \in \sY_{\text{in}}} P(y \mid \rvx)$.
\label{method:bg:msp}
\end{method}

We restrict attention to labels generable from $\rvx$ alone, through a labelling
function $f_{\rvy}$ that depends only on $\rvx$. Writing $\sY_{\text{all}}$ for the
labels producible over the full input space, a training subset need not realise all
of them, so in general $\sY_{\text{in}} \subsetneq \sY_{\text{all}}$. Two
distinctions recur. Methodologically, OOD detectors are either
\emph{training-agnostic}, applying to an existing classifier without altering its
training (as MSP does), or \emph{training-aware}, modifying architecture, loss, or
augmentation to improve detection. Conceptually, OOD detection targets
\emph{epistemic} uncertainty, the model's uncertainty from limited knowledge,
rather than the \emph{aleatoric} uncertainty intrinsic to noisy data.
This task is the setting for \Cref{ch:label-blindness,ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}.

\section{Anomaly Detection}
\label{sec:bg:anomaly}

Anomaly detection is adjacent to OOD detection and the two are often conflated, but
they differ in scope, training assumptions, and evaluation. Anomaly detection
identifies individual points that deviate from the expected pattern \emph{within} a
single distribution: rare events, noise, or fraud in the same domain as the
training data. OOD detection instead flags inputs from a \emph{different}
distribution altogether. The training assumption differs accordingly: anomaly
detection is typically a \emph{one-class} problem, with access only to normal data
at training time and no examples of the anomalies it must later reject, whereas OOD
detection usually operates over several supervised in-distribution classes. The
one-class framing recurs in this dissertation, in the unlabeled detectors of
\Cref{ch:label-blindness} and in the one-class contrastive probe of
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}, which treats faithful generations as the single
modelled class.

\section{Unlabeled Out-of-Distribution Detection}
\label{sec:bg:unlabeled}

Most OOD detectors are trained on labeled in-distribution data and repurpose a
supervised classifier's outputs, softmax confidence or logits, as the detection
signal. Not all do. We call a detector \emph{unlabeled} when its training uses only
the inputs and never the labels.

\begin{definition}[Unlabeled OOD Detection]
Let the in-distribution dataset be $\sD_{\text{in}} = \{(\rvx_i, \rvy_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$.
An OOD detector is \emph{unlabeled} if its training uses only the inputs
$\{\rvx_i\}$, producing a detection function
$f_{\text{OOD}} \gets \mathrm{Train}(\{\rvx_i\}_{i=1}^{N})$ with no dependence on the
labels $\{\rvy_i\}$.
\label{def:bg:unlabeled}
\end{definition}

Unlabeled detectors typically rest on unsupervised objectives such as density
estimation, reconstruction error, or self-supervised representations. A pretrained
model used without fine-tuning is itself a form of unlabeled detection, since it
never trains on the in-distribution labels. Such methods are attractive where labels
are expensive or where detection should be decoupled from any one classification
task. They still solve the same problem as labeled detection, estimating
$P(f_{\rvy}(\rvx) \notin \sY_{\text{in}})$, and the limits of that decoupling are
the subject of \Cref{ch:label-blindness}.

\section{Dataset Domain and the Single-Domain Setting}
\label{sec:bg:domain}

A dataset is drawn from a \emph{domain}: the environment, sensor, or generating
conditions under which it was collected. Formally, a domain labelling function
$f_{\rvd}$ assigns a domain label to each input, $\rvd = f_{\rvd}(\rvx)$. This
dissertation is concerned with the case in which all training classes share one
domain $\rvd_1$, so that $f_{\rvd}(\rvx) = \rvd_1$ for every in-distribution
$\rvx$; an input with $f_{\rvd}(\rvx) \neq \rvd_1$ can then be taken to lie outside
the in-distribution label set. Because every in-distribution class shares the
domain, we separate the features of $\rvx$ into \emph{domain features}
$\rvx_{\rvd}$, the minimal subset sufficient to infer the domain, and \emph{class
features} $\rvx_{\rvy}$, the minimal subset sufficient to infer the class. Within
the training set we take these to be independent, $I(\rvx_{\rvd} : \rvx_{\rvy}) = 0$,
though the independence need not hold over the full input space, where domain cues
can carry class information. Domains are hierarchical (cats $\subset$ mammals
$\subset$ animals), and at the top of the hierarchy the set of nontrivial domain
features is empty.

\begin{definition}[Single-Domain Dataset]
A dataset is \emph{single-domain} if there exists a nontrivial set of domain
features $\rvx_{\rvd}$ that are independent of the class features,
$I(\rvx_{\rvd} : \rvx_{\rvy}) = 0$, and non-vanishing in size,
$|\{\rvx_{\rvd}\}| \gg 0$. This distinguishes single-domain datasets from
multi-domain ones, where class diversity collapses the independent domain features
to approximately nothing, $|\{\rvx_{\rvd}\}| \approx 0$. In a single-domain
dataset, domain features capture global properties shared across all samples (the
imaging modality, the capture conditions) while class features capture the
discriminative properties used for labelling.
\label{def:bg:singledomain}
\end{definition}

Supervised training on a single-domain dataset induces a geometric failure mode in
the learned representation: because domain features carry no label signal, the
objective provides no gradient to preserve them, and the representation concentrates
its variance in the class-discriminative subspace, discarding the
domain-distinguishing directions an OOD shift would inhabit. That phenomenon,
\emph{Domain-Sensitivity Collapse}, and its consequences for distance-based
detection are the contribution of \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} and are not
treated as background primitives here; the geometric vocabulary needed to state it
is given in \Cref{sec:bg:geometry}.

\section{Large Language Models and Hallucination}
\label{sec:bg:llm}

The language-domain chapter operates on autoregressive language models and the
intermediate activations they expose.

\begin{definition}[Large Language Model]
A \emph{large language model} (LLM) is a parameterized distribution over token
sequences, $f_{\vtheta} : \mathcal{X}^{*} \to [0,1]$, where $\mathcal{X}$ is a
finite vocabulary and $\mathcal{X}^{*}$ the set of finite sequences over it. The
model factorizes the probability of a sequence $\vx = (x_1, \dots, x_T)$
autoregressively as
$\Pr_{\vtheta}(\vx) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \Pr_{\vtheta}(x_t \mid x_{<t})$, with
$x_{<t} = (x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$, and is parameterized by a deep network, typically
a Transformer, trained to minimize the next-token cross-entropy
$\E_{\vx \sim \mathcal{D}}[-\sum_{t} \log \Pr_{\vtheta}(x_t \mid x_{<t})]$ over a
corpus $\mathcal{D}$.
\label{def:bg:llm}
\end{definition}

The qualifier ``large'' reflects parameter counts in the billions and corpora of
hundreds of billions of tokens; at that scale, models exhibit in-context learning
and rich internal representations.

\begin{definition}[Hallucination]
Let $\vx$ be a prompt and $\vy$ a continuation sampled from
$\Pr_{\vtheta}(\cdot \mid \vx)$. A \emph{hallucination} occurs when $\vy$ contains
content not grounded in verifiable fact, contextually entailed information, or
available sources, relative to a reference world model $\mathcal{W}$: there exists a
span $\vy' \subseteq \vy$ that contradicts $\mathcal{W}$ or asserts unverifiable
content as fact.
\label{def:bg:hallucination}
\end{definition}

We distinguish two kinds. \emph{Extrinsic} hallucination contradicts an external
knowledge base $\mathcal{W}$, and is the failure mode this dissertation targets.
\emph{Intrinsic} hallucination violates internal consistency with the prompt or
earlier tokens. Note that grounding is relative to $\mathcal{W}$:
a factually outdated answer is wrong, not hallucinated, when $\mathcal{W}$ itself is
stale.

\paragraph{The residual stream.}
Detecting hallucination from a model's internals requires naming the object that
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} reads. A Transformer maintains, for each token
position, a sequence of intermediate activations $h_1, \dots, h_L$ across its $L$
layers, the \emph{residual stream}, with each $h_\ell \in \R^{d}$ the
$d$-dimensional hidden state written by layer $\ell$. These activations are
computed in a single forward pass and require no resampling, and they are the raw
material an internal-state detector scores. The detector of
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} learns from \emph{pairs} of such layer
activations. The information-theoretic justification for why a pair of layers should
share label-relevant information is built from the primitives of the next two
sections.

\section{Information Theory}
\label{sec:bg:infotheory}

Information theory~\citep{shannon1948mathematical} quantifies uncertainty and the
dependence between random variables, and supplies the first of the dissertation's
two lenses: a formal measure of what a representation retains about a target.

\begin{definition}[Entropy and Conditional Entropy]
The \emph{entropy} $H(\rvx)$ of a random variable $\rvx$ with distribution
$p(\vx)$ is its expected uncertainty. The \emph{conditional entropy}
$H(\rvy \mid \rvx)$ is the uncertainty remaining in $\rvy$ once $\rvx$ is known.
They satisfy the chain rule $H(\rvx, \rvy) = H(\rvx) + H(\rvy \mid \rvx)$.
\label{def:bg:entropy}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Mutual Information]
The \emph{mutual information} $I(\rvx; \rvy)$ is the information shared between
$\rvx$ and $\rvy$, the reduction in uncertainty about one given the other.
Equivalently it is the divergence of the joint from the product of marginals,
$I(\rvx; \rvy) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\vx, \vy) \,\|\, p(\vx)p(\vy))$, where
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is the Kullback--Leibler divergence. Mutual information is
non-negative, $I(\rvx; \rvy) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $\rvx$ and
$\rvy$ are independent, and it obeys the chain rule
$I(\rvx; \rvy) = I(\rvx; \rvz) + I(\rvy; \rvz \mid \rvx)$ relative to a mediating
variable $\rvz$.
\label{def:bg:mi}
\end{definition}

One consequence matters for the language-domain chapter: information cannot be
created by processing a variable.

\begin{definition}[Data Processing Inequality]
If three random variables form a Markov chain $\rvx \rightarrow \rvy \rightarrow \rvz$
(so $\rvz$ depends on $\rvx$ only through $\rvy$), then
$I(\rvx; \rvz) \le I(\rvx; \rvy)$: no transformation of $\rvy$ can increase its
information about $\rvx$.
\label{def:bg:dpi}
\end{definition}

The data processing inequality is what makes cross-layer mutual information
analysable in \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}: because later activations are
deterministic functions of earlier ones, mutual information measured between learned
embeddings lower-bounds the mutual information between the underlying layer
activations, so a positive measured value certifies a positive true value
(\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}, with the formal statement in
\Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs}).

\section{Information Bottleneck, Sufficiency, and Contrastive Estimation}
\label{sec:bg:ib}

The information-theoretic view becomes a tool for representation learning through
sufficiency and the information bottleneck, and it becomes \emph{operational}
through the contrastive bound that lets mutual information be estimated and
optimized.

\begin{definition}[Minimal Sufficient Statistic]
A statistic $T(\rvx)$ is \emph{sufficient} for $\rvy$ if it retains all the
information $\rvx$ carries about $\rvy$, i.e.\ $I(\rvx; \rvy \mid T(\rvx)) = 0$. It
is \emph{minimal} if it is a function of every other sufficient statistic; the
minimal sufficient statistic is thus the most compact representation that loses no
information about $\rvy$.
\label{def:bg:mss}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Information Bottleneck]
Given $\rvx$ and a target $\rvy$, the \emph{information bottleneck}~\citep{tishby2000information}
seeks a representation $\rvz$ of $\rvx$ that maximizes $I(\rvz; \rvy)$ while
minimizing $I(\rvz; \rvx)$, formalised as
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IB}} = I(\rvz; \rvy) - \beta\, I(\rvz; \rvx)$, where $\beta$
trades retention of label information against compression of the input. Under this
objective a learned representation is driven toward the minimal sufficient
statistic of \Cref{def:bg:mss}.
\label{def:bg:ib}
\end{definition}

Sufficiency drives the result of \Cref{ch:label-blindness}: when the only signal a
representation retains is independent of the label-relevant features, the minimal
sufficient statistic for the surrogate task carries nothing about the class, and
detection built on it must fail. The same machinery is constructive in the language
domain, but there mutual information must be \emph{estimated} from samples, which
the contrastive objective makes possible.

\begin{method}[InfoNCE Lower Bound]
Let two views $\mathrm{view}_a, \mathrm{view}_b$ be encoded and scored against
$K - 1$ negatives, and let $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}$ be the resulting InfoNCE
loss~\citep{oord2018representation}. It lower-bounds the views' mutual information,
$I(\mathrm{view}_a; \mathrm{view}_b) \ge \log K - \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}$;
equivalently, if the views share no information the loss cannot fall below its
floor, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} \ge \log K$.
\label{method:bg:infonce}
\end{method}

The InfoNCE bound is the canonical variational estimator of mutual information and
underlies modern contrastive representation learning, including its self-supervised
form over augmented views~\citep{chen2020simclr} and its supervised specialization,
which treats all same-class examples as mutual positives~\citep{khosla2020supervised}.
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} reads the bound in the contrapositive: a
contrastive loss that \emph{descends} below $\log K$ on a held-out split is itself
evidence that the target mutual information is positive. This is a feasibility
argument, whose formal version, with supervised contrast over layer-pair views, is
developed there and proved in \Cref{app:mi-hallucination-proofs}.

\section{Representation Geometry}
\label{sec:bg:geometry}

The second lens reads the same retained structure geometrically: as the
arrangement of learned features in space, and in particular which directions carry
variance and which are collapsed. Let $z = f_\theta(\rvx) \in \R^{d}$ be the
$d$-dimensional feature a model assigns to an input over a $C$-class dataset, and
let $\Sigma$ be its empirical covariance with eigenvalues
$\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$. The covariance decomposes into inter- and
intra-class parts,
$\Sigma = \Sigma_{\mathrm{between}} + \Sigma_{\mathrm{within}}$, where
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{between}}$ has rank at most $C - 1$; a representation that drives
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{within}} \to 0$ therefore concentrates essentially all variance
into a $(C-1)$-dimensional subspace.

\begin{definition}[Anisotropy: Participation Ratio and Effective Rank]
The concentration of a covariance spectrum is measured by the \emph{participation
ratio} and the \emph{effective rank}:
$\mathrm{PR}(\Sigma) = (\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2$ and
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\Sigma) = \exp(-\sum_i \hat{\lambda}_i \log \hat{\lambda}_i)$,
where $\hat{\lambda}_i = \lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j$. Both report how many
directions meaningfully carry variance; a representation with $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$
far below $d$ is strongly anisotropic.
\label{def:bg:anisotropy}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Class-Discriminative Subspace]
The \emph{class-discriminative subspace}
$\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cls}} = \mathrm{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is spanned by the
top-$k$ eigenvectors of $\Sigma$. With $P$ the orthogonal projector onto
$\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ and $P_\perp = I - P$, any feature splits as
$z = z_\parallel + z_\perp$ with $z_\parallel = Pz$ and $z_\perp = P_\perp z$; the
complementary directions $z_\perp$ are where a domain shift with little
in-distribution variance would register.
\label{def:bg:clssubspace}
\end{definition}

This geometry determines how detection scores behave. \emph{Distance-based} scores,
nearest-neighbour distance~\citep{sun2022out} and the Mahalanobis
score~\citep{lee2018simple}, weight directions by Euclidean coordinate differences,
so when the spectrum is concentrated they are dominated by the high-variance class
subspace and blind to the low-variance directions $z_\perp$ that may carry the
shift. \emph{Logit-based} scores, maximum softmax probability (\Cref{method:bg:msp})
and the energy score~\citep{liu2020energy}, read the same geometry through the
classifier head. The extreme of this concentration is a named geometry.

\begin{definition}[Neural Collapse]
\emph{Neural collapse}~\citep{Papyan2020nc} is the terminal-phase geometry in which
within-class variance vanishes, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{within}} \to 0$, the class means
form the vertices of a $(C-1)$-simplex equiangular tight frame, and the remaining
$d - (C-1)$ directions carry zero variance. It is optimal for classification and,
because it maps shift-distinguishing directions into the null space, adverse for
out-of-distribution detection.
\label{def:bg:neuralcollapse}
\end{definition}

Neural collapse is the asymptotic limit; \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}
characterizes the same anisotropy at any stage of training, states when it makes
distance-based detection fail, and gives a training-time remedy, with the formal
results in \Cref{app:dsc-proofs}. These geometric primitives, together with the
information-theoretic ones of \Cref{sec:bg:infotheory,sec:bg:ib}, are the two lenses
the remainder of the dissertation applies: \Cref{ch:literature-review} surveys their
use across vision OOD and language hallucination, and
\Cref{ch:label-blindness,ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse,ch:hallucination-detection}
deploy them.
