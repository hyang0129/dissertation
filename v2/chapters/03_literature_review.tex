\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature-review}

Detection depends on what a model represents about the distinction of interest.
Information theory describes the statistical dependence between a representation
and that distinction, while representation geometry describes how the relevant
features are organized across directions in feature space. This chapter reviews
how these perspectives developed in machine learning and how they shape two
application areas: out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and hallucination detection.
\Cref{ch:background} defines the formal tools used throughout the dissertation;
the focus here is how prior work uses them and which questions remain unresolved.

\section{Information Theory in Machine Learning}
\label{sec:lit:infotheory}

Information theory supplies a formal language for uncertainty and statistical
dependence. Entropy and mutual information originate with
\citet{shannon1948mathematical} and are developed in the standard reference of
\citet{cover1999elements}. Fano's inequality~\citep{robert1952fano} connects these
quantities to prediction error, while the infomax principle~\citep{linsker1988self}
proposes maximizing transmitted information as a representation-learning
objective. Together, these ideas make it possible to ask whether a learned feature
map retains information about a target, rather than only whether a particular
predictor succeeds.

\paragraph{The Information Bottleneck and Minimal Sufficiency.}
The information bottleneck~\citep{tishby2000information} frames representation
learning as a tradeoff between compressing the input and retaining information
about the target. It thereby connects compression to the minimal sufficient
statistic defined in \Cref{ch:background}. Its application to deep
networks~\citep{tishby2015deep} motivated the information-plane account of
training~\citep{shwartz2017opening}, in which networks first fit the data and then
compress their internal representations. Subsequent work showed that the observed
compression phase is not universal and depends on the architecture and its
nonlinearities~\citep{saxe2019information}.

Later work made the bottleneck useful beyond this descriptive debate. The
variational information bottleneck~\citep{alemi2017deep} provides a tractable
training objective. Minimality also yields invariance to nuisance
variables~\citep{achille2018emergence}, and the multi-view information
bottleneck~\citep{federici2020learning} connects sufficiency to contrastive
learning. Compression has also been related to changes in feature
geometry~\citep{shwartz2023compress}, providing an early connection to the
geometric literature reviewed in \Cref{sec:lit:representation}.

\paragraph{Estimating Mutual Information.}
Mutual information is difficult to estimate reliably for high-dimensional learned
features, so practical methods commonly optimize variational
bounds~\citep{poole2019variational}. MINE~\citep{belghazi2018mutual} learns a neural
critic for this purpose, while Deep InfoMax~\citep{hjelm2019learning} maximizes a
bound between local and global representations. InfoNCE
\citep{oord2018representation} supplies the lower bound used by many contrastive
methods. Under its standard sampling construction, a loss below the
zero-information value $\log K$ gives a strictly positive lower bound on the mutual
information between the paired views.

Information theory also connects representation learning to generalization.
Building on classical PAC bounds~\citep{mcallester1999pac},
\citet{xu2017information} bound generalization error using the information that a
learned hypothesis retains about its training sample. This literature establishes
both the promise and the limitation of the information-theoretic view: it can
state whether dependence remains in principle, but practical detection also
depends on how that information is encoded and estimated.

\section{Representation Learning, Geometry, and Probing}
\label{sec:lit:representation}

The information-theoretic view asks how much dependence a representation retains.
The geometric view asks how the learned features are arranged and whether a
downstream method can use the directions in which they vary. \Cref{ch:background}
defines the feature spectrum and effective rank as well as the class-discriminative
subspace. It also defines neural collapse. This section reviews how learning
objectives and architectures produce that geometry and how probing methods examine
it.

\paragraph{Architectures and Pretrained Representations.}
Convolutional networks established the modern visual representation
pipeline~\citep{lecun1998gradient,krizhevsky2012imagenet,he2016deep}. The
transformer~\citep{vaswani2017attention} and its vision
adaptations~\citep{dosovitskiy2020image,Touvron2021deit} extended the same basic
pipeline to sequence and patch representations. At larger scale, contrastive
vision--language models~\citep{radford2021learning} and self-supervised vision
backbones~\citep{oquab2023dinov2} provide general-purpose features. The language
side developed through pretrained and generative model families
\citep{radford2018improving,devlin2018bert,brown2020language,raffel2020exploring}.
These architectures matter here because their intermediate representations are
the inputs to the detectors studied later in the dissertation.

\paragraph{Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning.}
Self-supervised objectives learn representations without class labels. Early
methods used pretext tasks~\citep{gidaris2018unsupervised}. Instance
discrimination later produced SimCLR~\citep{chen2020simclr,chen2020simple}, while
MoCo~\citep{he2020momentum} introduced a momentum encoder.
DINO~\citep{Caron2021dino} and SimSiam~\citep{chen2021exploring} remove explicit
negative pairs. Masked
autoencoding~\citep{he2022masked} instead reconstructs hidden input patches.
Supervised contrastive
learning~\citep{khosla2020supervised} uses labels to define positive groups, while
the same view-alignment framework has been applied to sentence
embeddings~\citep{gao2021simcse}. Large-scale self-supervised features now serve as
general-purpose vision backbones~\citep{oquab2023dinov2}.

These methods extend a longer lineage of unsupervised representation learning.
Component analysis and factor models
\citep{pearson1901liii,hyvarinen2000independent,tipping1999probabilistic,bartholomew1987latent}
seek low-dimensional explanatory directions, while autoencoders
\citep{hinton2006reducing,vincent2008extracting,baldi2012autoencoders} learn an
encoding through reconstruction. Their objectives differ, but all determine which
features survive compression and therefore which distinctions remain available to
a detector.

\paragraph{The Geometry of Learned Features.}
Neural collapse~\citep{Papyan2020nc} describes an equiangular-tight-frame geometry
that emerges in the terminal phase of classifier training. Under the assumptions
of an unconstrained-feature model, this geometry is a global optimum of the
cross-entropy objective~\citep{Zhu2021nc}; related structure can persist under
transfer~\citep{Galanti2022collapse}. Dimensional or rank
collapse~\citep{Jing2022rank} describes the broader loss of feature directions.
For OOD detection, collapse-adjacent geometry can reduce the separation between
in-distribution and OOD features~\citep{Haas2022ncood}.

Contrastive learning provides a direct connection between objectives and geometry.
The alignment-and-uniformity analysis~\citep{wang2021understanding} characterizes
the same contrastive objective in terms of proximity between positive pairs and
coverage of the feature space. Thus, InfoNCE and related losses admit complementary
information-theoretic and geometric readings. Along with the
sufficiency--invariance connection~\citep{achille2018emergence} and analyses that
relate compression to collapse~\citep{shwartz2023compress}, this work explains why
the amount of retained information and its arrangement should be studied together
without treating them as identical quantities.

\paragraph{Probing Internal Representations.}
Probing asks what information an intermediate representation makes recoverable.
Linear classifiers trained on frozen layers provide the canonical diagnostic
method~\citep{alain2017understanding,belinkov2019analysis}. This approach has
identified linguistic structure in language models
\citep{tenney2019bert,hewitt2019structural} and specialized roles for individual
attention heads~\citep{voita2019analyzing}. Information-theoretic formulations
help distinguish information encoded by the model from information learned by an
overly expressive probe~\citep{voita2019information}. Concept- and circuit-level
interpretability~\citep{kim2018interpretability,olah2020zoom} and gradient-based
saliency~\citep{selvaraju2017grad} provide related ways to locate influential
features. These methods establish the methodological basis for asking where a
detectable signal resides inside a network.

\paragraph{Knowledge Distillation.}
Knowledge distillation~\citep{hinton2015distilling} transfers behavior or
representations from a teacher network to a student. Contrastive representation
distillation~\citep{tian2020crd} applies this idea to learned features, and
\citet{sun2020codir} extend it to language models. Contrastive distillation across
augmented views~\citep{zhang2022cds} provides a within-network variant. These
methods express teacher--student or view agreement through a mutual-information
objective. The same lineage supports two distinct uses later in the dissertation:
transferring domain-sensitive features to a visual student and distinguishing
cross-network distillation from cross-layer views within one language model.

\section{Out-of-Distribution Detection}
\label{sec:lit:ood}

OOD detection identifies inputs that fall outside the distributional or semantic
scope represented by the training data. The literature differs both in which OOD
regime it evaluates and in which part of a trained model supplies the detection
signal. These distinctions are central to the label-blindness analysis of
\Cref{ch:label-blindness} and the single-domain analysis of
\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}.

\paragraph{Problem Framing and Benchmarks.}
The generalized-OOD taxonomy~\citep{yang2021generalized} organizes several forms of
distribution shift, while OpenOOD
\citep{yang2022openood,zhang2023openood} standardizes evaluation across near- and
far-OOD settings so that methods can be compared under a common protocol despite
substantial differences in how much visual or semantic structure the two
distributions share. The broader open-set framing predates deep
learning~\citep{drummond2006open}. Adjacent OOD detection, introduced in
\Cref{ch:label-blindness}, isolates the high-overlap case in which held-out labels
cannot be separated using incidental dataset differences.

\paragraph{Post-Hoc Scorers.}
The dominant paradigm attaches a scoring function to a trained classifier. Output-
and logit-based methods begin with maximum softmax
probability~\citep{hendrycks2016baseline} and input
preprocessing~\citep{liang2017enhancing}. Later examples include the energy
score~\citep{liu2020energy} and generalized entropy~\citep{liu2023gen}.
Activation shaping~\citep{sun2021react,xuscaling} changes the intermediate
features, whereas logit sparsification~\citep{Sun2022dice} changes how they reach
the output. Related methods adapt the scoring framework to large label
spaces~\citep{huang2021mos}.

Feature-based methods instead measure the position of a test representation
relative to the training features. This family includes the class-conditional
Mahalanobis detector~\citep{lee2018simple}, self-supervised outlier
scoring~\citep{sehwag2021ssd}, the residual-space ViM
score~\citep{Wang2022vim}, the decision-boundary distance used by
fDBD~\citep{liu2024fdbd}, and nearest-neighbor
guidance~\citep{park2023nnguide}. Both families depend on the representation:
output-based methods see only directions used by the classifier head, while
feature-based methods can use only distinctions that remain in the feature space.

\paragraph{Unlabeled, Self-Supervised, and Zero-Shot Detection.}
A growing literature trains OOD detectors without using in-distribution class
labels. Self-supervised auxiliary tasks can improve robustness and OOD
sensitivity~\citep{hendrycks2019using,mohseni2020self}, while contrasting shifted
instances sharpens the learned score~\citep{tack2020csi}. Likelihood-based methods
can operate without class supervision~\citep{xiao2020likelihood}, and more recent
work studies unsupervised or training-data-free detection
\citep{liu2023unsupervised,du2024and,du2024does}. Vision--language models provide a
different access regime by enabling zero-shot detection from text
\citep{esmaeilpour2022zero,wang2023clipn}.

These methods should not be treated as one interchangeable family. Some learn from
in-distribution inputs, whereas others depend on a pretrained model or textual
class descriptions. A surrogate task introduces another source of supervision.
They nevertheless share a limitation when their score depends on a representation
learned without the target labels. If the surrogate task is independent of the
label-relevant features, high performance on that task cannot recover the
discarded label signal. Against this family, \Cref{ch:label-blindness} formalizes
the condition as a limitation theorem and introduces the Adjacent OOD benchmark
rather than another unlabeled score.

\paragraph{Single-Domain Detection, Collapse, and Transfer.}
Single-domain training presents a different problem. The labels can be available
even though the learned features lose directions that distinguish one domain from
another. Neural collapse can harm OOD separability~\citep{Haas2022ncood}, while
NECO~\citep{BenAmmar2024neco} turns neural-collapse geometry into a detection
score. Unlike collapse-based scorers such as NECO,
\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} identifies the suppression of directions
orthogonal to the class-discriminative subspace and restores them during training
rather than proposing another scorer. Pretrained features often provide strong OOD
baselines, but naive fine-tuning can distort their geometry~\citep{Kumar2022lp};
contrastive training can preserve some of that structure~\citep{Winkens2020contrastive}.
The problem is particularly important in specialized single domains such as medical
imaging~\citep{Berger2021medood,zhou2022rethinking}.

Ensemble and Bayesian methods provide an adjacent approach by estimating epistemic
uncertainty across models or parameter distributions
\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple,daxberger2019bayesian,pmlr-v235-xu24ae}.
Theoretical work on domain adaptation and OOD learning instead characterizes how
distribution shift changes the attainable error
\citep{ben2010theory,vyas2018out,katz2022training}. These approaches broaden the
uncertainty analysis, but they do not remove the need for a representation that
responds to the shift being detected.

\section{Hallucination Detection}
\label{sec:lit:hallucination}

Hallucination detection asks whether a language model's generation is faithful to
the relevant evidence or factual reference. It provides the language-model
counterpart to the representation question: which observable signals distinguish
unsupported generations from faithful ones? Existing methods read those signals
from the output distribution or from repeated generations. A separate family
examines the model's internal activations.

\paragraph{Definitions, Taxonomy, and Evaluation.}
Surveys organize hallucinations by their form and source~\citep{ji2022survey}, while
recent benchmarks distinguish detection from overall generation
quality~\citep{bang2025hallulens}. Evaluation targets are not uniform. Atomic-fact
scoring~\citep{min2023factscore} measures factual precision, curated hallucination
benchmarks~\citep{li2023halueval} test recognition or generation, and truthfulness
benchmarks~\citep{lin2021truthfulqa,lin2022truthfulqa} evaluate susceptibility to
common misconceptions. Fact verification~\citep{thorne2018fever} and tool- or
retrieval-augmented checkers~\citep{peng2023check,chern2023factool} introduce
external evidence. These resources operationalize related but distinct targets, so
generation correctness should not automatically be equated with factual support.

\paragraph{Output-Space Confidence Signals.}
Output-space detectors use signals from the model's predictions. Token likelihood
and predictive entropy are available from the generation pass, whereas explicit
self-evaluation scores such as $P(\mathrm{true})$~\citep{kadavath2022language}
require an additional evaluation pass but not repeated stochastic sampling. These
methods require no hidden-state access, but compress the model's uncertainty into a
scalar that may omit distinctions retained in intermediate activations.

\paragraph{Sampling- and Consistency-Based Detection.}
Sampling methods estimate uncertainty from variation across several generations.
SelfCheckGPT~\citep{manakul2023selfcheckgpt} measures the consistency of repeated
answers. Semantic entropy~\citep{farquhar2024detecting} first groups samples by
meaning and then measures uncertainty over the resulting clusters. Energy-based
refinements extend the approach beyond entropy~\citep{ma2025semantic}. These methods
can recover signals that a single output score misses, but they require several
forward passes. SEP~\citep{kossen2024semantic} transfers the semantic-entropy signal
into a single-pass activation probe, linking the sampling and internal-state
families.

\paragraph{Single-Layer Activation Probes.}
Internal-state methods apply the probing methodology of
\Cref{sec:lit:representation} to language-model activations. Linear and shallow
probes recover truthfulness from hidden states~\citep{azaria2023internal}, while
truth-correlated directions can be identified and manipulated
\citep{li2023iti}. True and false statements also separate geometrically in
activation space~\citep{marks2024geometry}. Other work elicits latent knowledge
without labeled truthfulness examples~\citep{burns2023discovering}. Together,
these results show that internal representations can retain distinctions that are
not explicit in the generated text or its scalar confidence.

\paragraph{Multi-Layer and Unsupervised Internal Signals.}
Recent probes use relationships across layers or larger activation structures.
Examples include layer-wise divergence~\citep{zhang2025icr}, cross-layer
attention~\citep{suresh2025clap}, ACT-ViT's classification of the full activation
tensor~\citep{barshalom2025actvit}, and EigenScore's analysis of internal-state
covariance~\citep{chen2024inside}. Other methods reduce the need for labeled probe
data. HaloScope learns from in-the-wild generations~\citep{du2024haloscope}, while
Lookback Lens uses attention maps alone~\citep{chuang2024lookback}. MIND monitors
internal states in real time~\citep{su2024mind}. This literature establishes
several ways to exploit internal state, but it leaves open how the choice of layers
and training objective should follow from a statement about the information
available between layers. Among these methods, ACT-ViT is the closest compute-matched
baseline; \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} instead derives a one-class contrastive
probe from cross-layer dependence and treats layer pairs from one frozen forward
pass as its views.

\section{Synthesis}

Across the OOD and hallucination literatures, detectors differ in what they can
observe. Some use classifier outputs or learned features; others use hidden
activations or repeated model behavior. They also differ in whether they can change
the representation or must operate on a frozen model. When a detector is restricted
to a representation that lacks the needed signal, improving the scorer alone cannot
recover it; the representation or the detector's inputs must change. When the needed
signal remains available, detector design determines whether it can be extracted.

The results chapters study each case separately. \Cref{ch:label-blindness} analyzes
a representation that lacks the target distinction,
\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} studies directions suppressed by
single-domain training, and \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} uses cross-layer
dependence to extract a signal retained in a frozen language model. The common
question is therefore not which detector is universally best, but whether the
representation available in a given setting contains the distinction that the
detector must make.
