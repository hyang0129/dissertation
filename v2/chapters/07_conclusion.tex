\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This dissertation started with a single question: when can a model's own
representation tell us the model is about to fail? The systems we deploy keep
meeting inputs their training never covered, and in those moments what protects a
user is not the model's accuracy but its ability to flag the cases it cannot
handle. Out-of-distribution detection and hallucination detection are two versions
of that flagging problem. The dissertation's claim is that one condition governs
both: a shift is detectable when the representation a model has learned still
carries the structure that sets the shift apart. A representation that never
encoded that structure leaves a detector nothing to find. A representation that had
it and lost it in training can be made to carry it again. When the representation
keeps it, a detector can read it out.

Two measurements made that condition usable. Mutual information puts a number on
how much a representation still says about the distinction we need; the geometry of
the feature space shows the same shortfall as variance that has drained out of the
shift directions. We did not choose between them. Each chapter
used the measurement that stated its case more plainly, and across the three the
picture is one of degree, of how much distinguishing structure the representation
has left. \Cref{ch:label-blindness} works at the floor, where the structure is gone
and no detector can win. \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} works one step up,
where training has drained structure the data had supplied, and shows how to refill
it. \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} works where the model has held on to the
structure, and reads the shift straight off it. What follows pulls the three
results into one account, says without hedging what they do and do not show, and
lists the questions they leave open.

\section{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:concl:summary}

The contributions sit along the spine just sketched. We take the three studies one
at a time and then say what joins them, since the dissertation's main claim is that
they form a single argument and not three separate ones.

\subsection{Contributions by Study}
\label{subsec:concl:per-pillar}

\paragraph{Label blindness: a guaranteed-failure result for unlabeled
detection (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}).}
The first study asks whether an out-of-distribution detector can be built without
labels. In the general case it cannot. If the self- or unsupervised surrogate task
is independent of the label-relevant features, the minimal sufficient statistic the
model learns holds nothing about the label distinction, and detection against that
distinction is \emph{guaranteed to fail} (\Cref{mainbodyfailood}). This is the one
place in the dissertation where we prove an impossibility instead of diagnosing a
difficulty. The result is not that current methods happen to score badly; it is
that no method resting on a label-independent objective can work in the worst case.
The conditions that trigger the failure are not exotic either. They arise in
ordinary deployments (\Cref{mainbodyoverlap}). To make the failure visible we built
the \emph{Adjacent OOD} benchmark, which forces the feature overlap that standard
near/far benchmarks leave out, and that overlap is exactly what a label-blind
detector cannot survive.

\paragraph{Domain-sensitivity collapse: a diagnosis and a recovery
(\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}).}
The second study moves to single-domain models, where the failure is not built in
but brought on by training. Supervised training on one domain pulls the features
into a single low-rank subspace aligned with the classes, leaving the domain-shift
directions flat, so distance-based and logit-based scorers stop
reacting to shifts they ought to catch. We call this \emph{domain-sensitivity collapse} (DSC) and
account for it geometrically, with bounds that say where distance scores
(\Cref{thm:dsc:dist-fail}) and logit scores (\Cref{prop:dsc:msp-insens}) lose their
grip. The bounds are diagnostic. They predict and explain the failure; unlike the
label-blindness result they do not declare it permanent, and \emph{Teacher-Guided
Training} (TGT, \Cref{sec:dsc:method:tgt}) reverses it. TGT distills the
class-suppressed residual of a frozen multi-domain teacher into the student during
training and so restores the domain-sensitive geometry, at no inference-time cost.

\paragraph{Hallucination detection: a detector from a first principle
(\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}).}
The third study shows that hallucination in a large language model can be detected
from the representation the model already computes. The argument is constructive.
Because a transformer's residual stream carries information forward deterministically,
the mutual information between two of its layers is positive, and an InfoNCE
objective cannot fall below its information floor unless the signal it chases is
really present. So the bare fact that the probe trains shows the signal exists.
On that basis we train a one-class contrastive probe that reads pairs of
cross-layer activations (\Cref{sec:hd:method}). It \emph{matches-or-outperforms, in the
mean,} the strongest engineered probe (ACT-ViT;~\citealp{barshalom2025actvit}) over
the models and datasets we ran (\Cref{sec:hd:results}). We do not read this as a
leaderboard win. The probe reaches \emph{parity} with the best learned baseline
rather than beating it, and parity is what the argument predicts: a detector that
follows from a correct account of where the signal lives should land in the same
band as one tuned hard against the benchmark, while a detector built on a wrong
account should not. The theory also stakes a \emph{falsifiable} claim, that
symmetric supervision should break down when one class has no coherent latent
structure (\Cref{cor:hd:symcon}), and the ablation breaks down just as it says.

\subsection{Synthesis: Three Studies, One Thesis}
\label{subsec:concl:synthesis}

Taken separately, the three studies have little in common: a vision benchmark in
one, a histopathology model in another, a language model at the end. What makes them
one dissertation is that each puts the same question to its own setting. Has the
representation kept what tells the shift apart? They differ only in the answer,
because each sits at a different point on a single arc.

That arc is more than the order of the chapters. It is a scale of how much the
representation offers a detector. \Cref{ch:label-blindness} sits at the end of the
scale where nothing is offered: the distinguishing structure is missing by
construction, so detection fails for want of any signal, not for want of a cleverer
score. \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} sits a little way in. The structure was
in the data, training threw it out, and we show both why that happens and how to
undo it. \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} sits where the model has held the
structure in its intermediate activations, and the work is to pull it back out.
Detectability rises along the scale as more of the structure survives.

Asking one question across such different settings is possible only because it can
be posed in two languages. Label blindness and the hallucination result are stated
information-theoretically: the label-relevant signal is mutual information, and the
shift stays detectable only while that quantity is above zero, a point the
data-processing inequality (\Cref{def:bg:dpi}) pins down in both. Domain-sensitivity
collapse is stated geometrically: the signal is variance along the domain-shift
directions, and detection fails once those directions fold into the class subspace
(\Cref{def:bg:clssubspace}). These are not two findings but one seen twice: throwing
away mutual information and flattening a set of directions are the same act. Reaching
for the sharper vocabulary in each study exposed the
condition the three share, and that condition, one test posed in either language,
stands behind all three failures the dissertation took apart. It is the central
contribution.

\section{Broader Implications}
\label{sec:concl:implications}

The three results carry past their immediate settings into how reliable machine
learning gets built and judged.

\subsection{AI Safety and Reliability}
\label{subsec:concl:safety}

Most work on the reliability of deployed models runs empirically: propose a
detector, score it on a benchmark, keep it if the number looks good. This
dissertation argues for a second discipline beside that one, which asks whether an
entire family of methods can work at all, ahead of asking how well any single method
scores.
Knowing that a label-independent detector is bound to fail in the overlap regime
(\Cref{ch:label-blindness}) is, on its own, a contribution to safety. It warns a
practitioner off unlabeled detection wherever in- and out-of-distribution data share
features, however well that detector did on a benchmark that kept them apart. The
other two studies carry the same kind of warning. A diagnosis of domain-sensitivity
collapse tells the teams who run narrow-domain models, in clinical imaging or remote
sensing or any pipeline built on a single sensor, why a distance- or logit-based
monitor may be quietly missing shifts, and it hands them a fix that costs nothing
once the model is live. Locating the hallucination signal inside a model's
activations gives factuality-critical applications a way to check a generation
without paying for extra samples. None of these is a benchmark gain. Each is an
account of how the failure works, and a reliability check whose guarantees we
understand earns more trust than one backed only by the benchmarks it was lucky
enough to be tried on~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple}.

\subsection{Information Theory and Representation Geometry in Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:concl:lenses}

The method carries a lesson of its own. ``What does the representation preserve?''
travels well, and it can be put in either of two languages. Information theory gave
us the impossibility result and the feasibility argument; geometry gave us the
diagnosis and the recovery. The synthesis above showed the two tracking one
quantity. The data-processing inequality, the variational bounds on mutual
information, and the geometric measures of effective rank and subspace collapse were
not built for these particular problems. They are general instruments for reasoning
about reliability, and part of what the dissertation does is show how far they
reach. The neural-collapse geometry behind single-domain failure
(\Cref{def:bg:neuralcollapse}) and the cross-layer information behind hallucination
detection are the same kind of claim about preserved structure, and seeing them that
way knits the work together.

\subsection{Evaluation and Benchmarking}
\label{subsec:concl:eval}

A theme runs through all three studies: a benchmark should be designed to bring out
the failures a theory predicts, not chosen because it is convenient. The Adjacent
OOD benchmark (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}) exists because the standard near/far
benchmarks dodge the overlap regime where label blindness shows up, and so hand out
reassuring numbers for methods the theory expects to fail in the field. The
single-domain evaluation behind domain-sensitivity collapse closes a similar gap,
between lab habit, which leans toward multi-domain data, and deployment, which is
often narrow. The wider point is that how you evaluate is itself a choice with
consequences. A benchmark that cannot even express a failure will never flag it, and
a method is only as trustworthy as the conditions its benchmarks actually reach.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl:limitations}

It is worth being clear about how far the claims go and where they stop. The thesis
has two halves. The work marks out \emph{when} detection is possible, and it shows
that principled methods get to that boundary. It does not show those methods are
optimal, or that the idealized conditions hold without slack.

\paragraph{Label blindness.}
The guaranteed-failure result rests on an idealization, \emph{strict} statistical
independence between the surrogate objective and the label-relevant features. Real
self-supervised objectives are seldom independent of the label distinction in that
clean a way. They are partly aligned with it, so the theorem bounds a worst case and
does not forecast exact field numbers. The Adjacent OOD benchmark builds one regime
of high feature overlap. It is a sharp probe for the failure we predict, but it is a
single constructed regime, not the full range of shifts a deployed system will see.

\paragraph{Domain-sensitivity collapse.}
The bounds of \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} are diagnostic and should not be
read as guarantees. They say where scorers lose sensitivity and why, but they do not
prove recovery is impossible without help, and TGT in fact recovers it. TGT also
inherits whatever its teacher covers. It distills a frozen multi-domain teacher, so
the geometry it can restore reaches only as far as that teacher's domains. The
empirical results span several single-domain benchmarks, which is not the same as
covering every modality or application.

\paragraph{Hallucination detection.}
The detector reaches \emph{parity} with the strongest engineered baseline rather
than passing it, and the parity is uneven. It wins cleanly on some model-dataset
cells, ties on others, and loses outright on the Natural Questions cells, which we
report in full (\Cref{sec:hd:results}). The feasibility-by-trainability argument
shows the label-relevant signal \emph{is present} in the cross-layer representation.
It does not show that this particular probe reads that signal out optimally, and a
better readout may exist. The evaluation covers two open-weights model families at
the $8$B scale and a fixed set of question-answering datasets
(\Cref{sec:hd:limitations}). How the method behaves on larger models, on closed
models, and on other task formats is bounded by what we were able to test.

\paragraph{The thesis, kept in scope.}
One limitation is common to all three studies and is worth stating once. The
dissertation charts the limit of what can be detected and shows that principled
methods reach it. It does not claim those methods are the best available at that
limit, or that the idealized conditions hold exactly in every deployment. Holding
that line keeps the limitations from crowding out the contribution: the
contribution is the \emph{condition}, and the evidence that it can be met, not a
claim to optimality.

\section{Future Directions}
\label{sec:concl:future}

The limitations are not dead ends; each opens a concrete problem. We pick out three
that come straight out of the work and that we did not settle.

\paragraph{Testing for label blindness \emph{a priori}.}
As it stands the label-blindness theorem is a diagnosis: given a surrogate
objective, one can argue about whether it is independent of the label distinction.
The more useful tool would \emph{predict} blindness before any training is done. It
would be an estimable quantity, read off an objective and a label set, that flags
when a self-supervised method is at risk for a given deployment. A test like that
would turn a worst-case theorem into a screening step, and it would speak to the
idealization above by measuring \emph{how} independent a real objective is instead
of assuming the independence is exact.

\paragraph{An information-theoretic dual for domain-sensitivity collapse.}
We developed domain-sensitivity collapse deliberately in geometric terms, and the
synthesis of \Cref{subsec:concl:synthesis} claims its geometric statement and an
information-theoretic one ought to coincide. Making that coincidence formal, by
deriving the variance-suppression account of DSC from a mutual-information argument
or the reverse, would do more than tidy the theory. It would test the dissertation's
central claim that the two languages describe one quantity, and it would test it on
the one study where so far a single language has done all the work.

\paragraph{From detection to mitigation, and across model families.}
The cross-layer probe detects hallucination from a model's activations. The obvious
next question is whether the same signal can \emph{steer} generation away from
hallucinating, which would turn the detector into a mitigation. Transfer is just as
open: whether the construction survives across model families and at scales past the
ones we ran, larger and closed models included. Both questions fall out of the scope
limits of \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}, and both would show whether
feasibility-by-trainability generalizes as neatly as the theory hopes.

\section{Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:concl:remarks}

Whether a machine learning system can be trusted outside its training comes down to
its representation. Does that representation still hold the structure that would let
the system notice the world has moved? The dissertation has argued that one
condition settles this across problems as far apart as unlabeled out-of-distribution
detection and language-model hallucination, with information theory and geometry
offering two routes to the same check. We proved where the structure cannot be
there, put it back where training had stripped it out, and read it off where the
model had kept it. The methods are not the final word, and the conditions that mark
the limit are cleaner than any real deployment. The limit itself is real, though,
and it is the same one in every study: a detector can only find a shift that the
representation has kept the means to tell apart.
