\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This dissertation began from a single question: when can a learned representation
support the detection of its own failures? Modern machine learning systems are
deployed in settings their training never anticipated, and their usefulness in
those settings depends less on raw accuracy than on the ability to recognize when
an input, or an output, falls outside what the model can be trusted to handle.
Out-of-distribution detection and hallucination detection are two faces of this
reliability problem. The thesis defended here is that both are governed by the
same underlying condition: \emph{detection is possible exactly when the learned
representation preserves the structure that distinguishes the shift.} Where that
structure is absent, no scoring rule can recover it; where training has suppressed
it, the structure can be restored; and where the representation retains it, a
principled detector can read it out.

We pursued that thesis with two complementary instruments. Information theory
measures what a representation preserves as mutual information---how much the
features still tell us about the distinction we care about. Representation
geometry measures the same preservation as structure in feature space---which
directions carry variance, which subspaces have collapsed. These are not two
topics but two lenses on one quantity, and the three studies that form the body of
this dissertation deploy them in sequence across a single arc: \emph{failure,
recovery, detection.} \Cref{ch:label-blindness} establishes a regime in which the
representation provably cannot support detection; \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}
diagnoses a regime in which training destroys structure that was available, and
restores it; and \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} detects a shift by reading the
structure a model already preserves. This closing chapter draws those three
results together, states honestly what they do and do not establish, and sets out
the open problems they leave behind.

\section{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:concl:summary}

The dissertation makes contributions along the spine described above. We first
summarize each study on its own terms, then---because the central claim of this
work is that the three are one argument rather than three---draw out the synthesis
that binds them.

\subsection{Contributions by Study}
\label{subsec:concl:per-pillar}

\paragraph{Label blindness: a guaranteed-failure result for unlabeled
detection (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}).}
The first study asks whether labels can be ignored when building an
out-of-distribution detector, and answers, formally, that they cannot in general.
When the self- or unsupervised surrogate task is statistically independent of the
label-relevant features, the learned minimal sufficient statistic carries no
information about the label distinction, and out-of-distribution detection against
that distinction is \emph{guaranteed to fail}
(\Cref{mainbodyfailood}). This is the dissertation's one genuine impossibility
result: not a statement that existing methods perform poorly, but a proof that no
method built on a label-independent objective can succeed in the worst case. We
further show that the conditions under which this failure bites are unavoidable in
realistic deployments (\Cref{mainbodyoverlap}), and we introduce the
\emph{Adjacent OOD} benchmark, which constructs the overlap regime that exposes
the failure---a regime existing near/far benchmarks systematically avoid, and
which therefore masks the very weakness practitioners most need to see.

\paragraph{Domain-sensitivity collapse: a diagnosis and a recovery
(\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}).}
The second study turns to single-domain models, where the failure is not
fundamental but \emph{induced}. Supervised training on a single domain drives
features into a low-rank, class-aligned subspace and suppresses the directions
along which domain shift would register, so distance-based and logit-based scorers
lose sensitivity to exactly the shifts they are meant to catch. We name this
phenomenon \emph{domain-sensitivity collapse} (DSC) and give it a geometric
account, with diagnostic bounds that predict where distance scores
(\Cref{thm:dsc:dist-fail}) and logit scores (\Cref{prop:dsc:msp-insens}) degrade.
We are deliberate that these are \emph{diagnostic} bounds, not impossibility
guarantees: they explain and predict a failure mode, but---unlike label
blindness---they do not assert that detection is unrecoverable. Indeed, the study's
second contribution is a recovery. \emph{Teacher-Guided Training} (TGT,
\Cref{sec:dsc:method:tgt}) distills a frozen multi-domain teacher's
class-suppressed residual structure into the model during training, restoring
domain-sensitive geometry with no inference-time overhead. The collapse that
training caused, training can be made to undo.

\paragraph{Hallucination detection: a detector from a first principle
(\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}).}
The third study shows that hallucination in large language models is
\emph{detectable} from the representation the model itself computes. The
information-theoretic argument is constructive: cross-layer mutual information in
the residual stream is positive by the data-processing structure of the
transformer, and an InfoNCE objective cannot descend below its information floor
unless that signal exists---so the trainability of the probe is itself the
evidence that the label-relevant signal is present. On this principle we build a
one-class contrastive probe over cross-layer activation pairs
(\Cref{sec:hd:method}), and it \emph{matches-or-outperforms, in the mean,} the
strongest engineered probe (ACT-ViT;~\citealp{barshalom2025actvit}) across the
evaluated models and datasets (\Cref{sec:hd:results}). We do not present this as a
leaderboard victory: the method reaches \emph{parity} with the best learned
baseline rather than dominating it, and that parity is the point. A detector
derived from a first principle that lands in the same performance band as a
heavily engineered one is evidence that the principle is correct---a wrong account
of where the signal lives would not arrive there. The study also delivers a
\emph{falsifiable} prediction, confirmed: the theory anticipates that symmetric
supervision should fail when one class lacks coherent latent structure
(\Cref{cor:hd:symcon}), and the corresponding ablation degrades as predicted.
Theory predicted; ablation confirmed.

\subsection{Synthesis: Three Studies, One Thesis}
\label{subsec:concl:synthesis}

Taken in isolation, the three studies address different tasks, different data, and
different model families. What makes them a single dissertation rather than three
papers is that each is the same question---\emph{does the representation preserve
what distinguishes the shift?}---asked at a different point along one arc.

The arc is \emph{failure, recovery, detection}, and it is not merely a narrative
ordering; it is a progression in what the representation makes available.
\Cref{ch:label-blindness} marks the boundary of the possible: it identifies the
regime in which the distinguishing structure is \emph{absent} from the
representation by construction, so that detection fails not for want of a better
score but for want of any signal to score. \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}
sits just inside that boundary: the structure was available in the data but
training \emph{destroyed} it, and the contribution is to show both why this happens
and how to put the structure back. \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} operates where
the structure is \emph{retained}: the model preserves the distinction in its
intermediate activations, and the task is to read it out faithfully. Absent,
suppressed, retained---the three studies tile the space of what a representation
can preserve, and the detectability of the shift tracks that preservation exactly.

The two lenses are what let the same question be asked in settings this different.
Label blindness and hallucination detection are stated in the language of
information theory: the label-relevant signal is mutual information, and detection
is feasible precisely when that mutual information is non-zero---a condition the
data-processing inequality (\Cref{def:bg:dpi}) makes precise in both. Domain-sensitivity
collapse is stated in the language of geometry: the signal is variance along
domain-shift directions, and detection degrades precisely when those directions
collapse into the class subspace (\Cref{def:bg:clssubspace}). These descriptions
are duals, not rivals. The information a representation discards and the geometric
directions it flattens are two accounts of one event---a loss of preserved
structure---and the dissertation's choice to read each study through whichever lens
is sharper for it is what reveals the common condition beneath three superficially
unrelated failures. That a single condition, expressed through two
interchangeable lenses, governs unlabeled OOD detection, single-domain OOD
detection, and LLM hallucination detection alike is the central contribution of
this work.

\section{Broader Implications}
\label{sec:concl:implications}

The contributions above have implications beyond their immediate technical
settings, for how reliable machine learning is built, analyzed, and evaluated.

\subsection{AI Safety and Reliability}
\label{subsec:concl:safety}

Much work on the reliability of deployed models is empirical: a detector is
proposed, measured on a benchmark, and adopted if it scores well. This dissertation
argues for a complementary, foundational discipline---understanding \emph{when} a
class of methods can work at all, before asking how well a particular instance
performs. Knowing that a label-independent detector is guaranteed to fail in the
overlap regime (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}) is itself a safety contribution: it
tells a practitioner not to trust unlabeled detection where in- and
out-of-distribution data share features, however well it scored on a
non-overlapping benchmark. The same posture runs through the other two studies.
Diagnosing domain-sensitivity collapse tells practitioners deploying
narrow-domain models---in medical imaging, remote sensing, industrial
inspection---why their distance- and logit-based monitors may be quietly
insensitive, and gives them a training-time remedy that costs nothing at
inference. Locating hallucination signal in a model's own activations offers a
detection route for factuality-critical applications that needs no sampling
overhead. In each case the safety value comes from a principled account of the
failure mode, not from an incremental benchmark gain. Reliability mechanisms whose
guarantees are understood are more trustworthy than those validated only by the
benchmarks they happen to have been tested on~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple}.

\subsection{Information Theory and Representation Geometry in Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:concl:lenses}

The methodological lesson of this work is that ``what does the representation
preserve?'' is a portable and productive question, and that it can be asked in two
interchangeable languages. Information theory supplied the impossibility result and
the feasibility argument; geometry supplied the diagnosis and the recovery; and the
synthesis above shows the two describing the same underlying quantity. The
data-processing inequality, the variational mutual-information bounds, and the
geometric notions of effective rank and subspace collapse are not specialized
tools for the three problems studied here---they are general instruments for
reasoning about reliability, and this dissertation is in part a demonstration of
their reach. The neural-collapse geometry that explains single-domain failure
(\Cref{def:bg:neuralcollapse}) and the cross-layer mutual information that licenses
hallucination detection are, at bottom, the same kind of statement about preserved
structure, and treating them as such is what unifies the work.

\subsection{Evaluation and Benchmarking}
\label{subsec:concl:eval}

A recurring theme is that benchmarks should be built to expose theoretically
predicted failure modes, not merely to be convenient. The Adjacent OOD benchmark
(\Cref{ch:label-blindness}) exists precisely because standard near/far benchmarks
avoid the overlap regime in which label blindness manifests, and so report
optimistic numbers for methods that a theory predicts will fail in deployment.
Likewise, the single-domain evaluation underlying domain-sensitivity collapse
addresses a gap between laboratory practice, which favors multi-domain settings,
and real deployment, which is frequently narrow-domain. The broader implication is
that evaluation design is not neutral: a benchmark that cannot express a predicted
failure cannot detect it, and the community's confidence in a method is only as
sound as the regimes its benchmarks actually probe.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl:limitations}

The honest scope of this dissertation is narrower than its ambitions, and stating
the limits plainly is part of defending the claims. The unifying thesis is that
this work establishes \emph{when} detection is possible and \emph{that} principled
methods reach that frontier---not that the methods presented are optimal, nor that
the conditions hold without idealization.

\paragraph{Label blindness.}
The guaranteed-failure result rests on an idealization: \emph{strict} statistical
independence between the surrogate objective and the label-relevant features. Real
self-supervised objectives are rarely perfectly independent of the label
distinction; they are partially aligned, and the theorem therefore bounds a worst
case rather than predicting exact field performance. The Adjacent OOD benchmark
constructs one regime of high feature overlap; it is a sharp instrument for the
predicted failure, but it is one constructed regime, not the entire space of shifts
a deployed system may face.

\paragraph{Domain-sensitivity collapse.}
The bounds of \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} are diagnostic, and must not be
read as guarantees: they explain and predict where scorers lose sensitivity, but
they do not prove that recovery is impossible without intervention---and indeed
TGT recovers it. The recovery method itself inherits the coverage of its teacher:
TGT distills a frozen multi-domain teacher, and the geometry it can restore is
bounded by the domains that teacher represents. The empirical validation, while
spanning several single-domain benchmarks, is not exhaustive across modalities or
application areas.

\paragraph{Hallucination detection.}
The hallucination detector reaches \emph{parity} with the strongest engineered
baseline rather than surpassing it, and the parity is uneven: the method wins
clearly on some dataset-model cells, ties on others, and---reported, not
hidden---loses firmly on the Natural Questions cells (\Cref{sec:hd:results}). The
feasibility-by-trainability argument establishes that the label-relevant signal
\emph{exists} in the cross-layer representation; it does not establish that this
particular probe extracts it optimally, and a better readout may exist. The
evaluation covers two open-weights model families at the $8$B scale and a fixed set
of question-answering datasets (\Cref{sec:hd:limitations}); transfer to larger
models, to closed models, and to other task formats is bounded by what was tested.

\paragraph{The thesis, scoped honestly.}
Across all three studies the common limitation is the same, and it is worth stating
as one: this dissertation maps the frontier of detectability and shows that
principled methods reach it. It does not claim that the methods are the best
possible at that frontier, nor that the idealized conditions delineating the
frontier hold exactly in every deployment. Keeping that distinction crisp is what
prevents the limitations from undercutting the spine---the contribution is the
\emph{condition} and the demonstration that it can be met, not a claim of
optimality.

\section{Future Directions}
\label{sec:concl:future}

The limitations above are not dead ends; each points to a concrete open problem.
We highlight three that follow directly from the work and that this dissertation
does not answer.

\paragraph{Testing for label blindness \emph{a priori}.}
The label blindness theorem currently functions as a diagnosis: given a surrogate
objective, one can reason about whether it is independent of the label distinction.
A more useful tool would \emph{predict} blindness before training---an estimable
quantity, computed from an objective and a label set, that signals whether a
self-supervised method is at risk of label blindness for a given deployment. Such a
test would convert the worst-case theorem into a practical screening procedure and
would directly address the idealization noted above, by quantifying \emph{how}
independent a real objective is rather than assuming strict independence.

\paragraph{An information-theoretic dual for domain-sensitivity collapse.}
Domain-sensitivity collapse was developed deliberately in geometric terms, and the
synthesis of \Cref{subsec:concl:synthesis} argues that its geometric statement and
an information-theoretic one should be duals. Making that duality formal---deriving
the variance-suppression account of DSC from a mutual-information argument, or vice
versa---would do more than tidy the theory: it would test the dissertation's
central claim that the two lenses describe one quantity, on the one study where
only a single lens has so far been applied.

\paragraph{From detection to mitigation, and across model families.}
The cross-layer probe detects hallucination from a model's activations; a natural
next step is to ask whether the same signal can be used to \emph{steer} generation
away from hallucination, turning a detector into a mitigation mechanism. Equally
open is the question of transfer: whether the construction holds across model
families and scales beyond those evaluated here, including larger and closed
models. Both questions follow directly from the scope limits of
\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}, and both would test whether the
feasibility-by-trainability principle generalizes as cleanly as the theory
suggests it should.

\section{Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:concl:remarks}

The reliability of a machine learning system in the open world is, at bottom, a
question about its representation: does it still carry the structure that would let
it recognize when something has changed? This dissertation has argued that a single
condition---the preservation of distinguishing structure---governs reliability
across settings as different as unlabeled out-of-distribution detection,
single-domain detection, and large language model hallucination, and that two
lenses, information theory and geometry, are interchangeable ways of asking whether
that condition holds. Along one arc from failure to recovery to detection, we
proved where the structure cannot be present, restored it where training had
removed it, and read it out where the model preserved it. The methods are not the
last word at the frontier they map, and the conditions that draw the frontier are
idealizations of a messier reality. But the frontier is real, and it is the same
frontier in every case. A system that is to be trusted beyond its training must
preserve what distinguishes the world it will meet from the world it was shown---and
whether it does is a question we now know how to ask.
