\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This dissertation began from one question: when can a learned representation support
the detection of its own failures? Machine learning systems are deployed in settings
their training never anticipated, and in those settings what matters is less raw
accuracy than the ability to recognize when an input, or an output, falls outside
what the model can handle. Out-of-distribution detection and hallucination detection
are both instances of this reliability problem. We have argued that one condition
governs both: \emph{detection is possible exactly when the learned representation
preserves the structure that distinguishes the shift.} When that structure is
absent, no scoring rule can recover it. When training has suppressed it, it can be
restored. When the representation keeps it, a detector can read it out.

We pursued that thesis with two tools. Information theory measures what a
representation preserves as mutual information: how much the features still tell us
about the distinction we care about. Representation geometry measures the same thing
as structure in feature space: which directions carry variance, and which have
collapsed. The two describe one quantity, and the three studies use them in turn
along a single arc: \emph{failure, recovery, detection.} \Cref{ch:label-blindness}
establishes a regime in which the representation provably cannot support detection.
\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} diagnoses a regime in which training destroys
structure that was available, and restores it. \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}
detects a shift by reading the structure a model already keeps. This chapter draws
the three results together, states plainly what they do and do not establish, and
sets out the open problems they leave behind.

\section{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:concl:summary}

The dissertation makes contributions along the spine described above. We summarize
each study on its own terms, and then draw out the synthesis that binds them, since
the central claim is that the three are one argument rather than three.

\subsection{Contributions by Study}
\label{subsec:concl:per-pillar}

\paragraph{Label blindness: a guaranteed-failure result for unlabeled
detection (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}).}
The first study asks whether labels can be ignored when building an
out-of-distribution detector. The answer, formally, is that they cannot in general.
When the self- or unsupervised surrogate task is independent of the label-relevant
features, the learned minimal sufficient statistic carries no information about the
label distinction, and detection against that distinction is \emph{guaranteed to
fail} (\Cref{mainbodyfailood}). This is the dissertation's one genuine impossibility
result. It is not a claim that existing methods score poorly; it is a proof that no
method built on a label-independent objective can succeed in the worst case. We also
show that the conditions for this failure are unavoidable in realistic deployments
(\Cref{mainbodyoverlap}), and we introduce the \emph{Adjacent OOD} benchmark, which
builds the overlap regime that exposes it. Standard near/far benchmarks avoid that
regime, so they hide the weakness practitioners most need to see.

\paragraph{Domain-sensitivity collapse: a diagnosis and a recovery
(\Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}).}
The second study turns to single-domain models, where the failure is not fundamental
but \emph{induced}. Supervised training on a single domain drives features into a
low-rank, class-aligned subspace and suppresses the directions along which a domain
shift would register, so distance-based and logit-based scorers lose sensitivity to
the shifts they should catch. We name this phenomenon \emph{domain-sensitivity
collapse} (DSC) and give it a geometric account, with diagnostic bounds that predict
where distance scores (\Cref{thm:dsc:dist-fail}) and logit scores
(\Cref{prop:dsc:msp-insens}) degrade. These are \emph{diagnostic} bounds, not
impossibility guarantees. They explain and predict a failure mode, but, unlike label
blindness, they do not claim that detection is unrecoverable. The study's second
contribution is the recovery. \emph{Teacher-Guided Training} (TGT,
\Cref{sec:dsc:method:tgt}) distills the class-suppressed residual structure of a
frozen multi-domain teacher into the model during training, which restores
domain-sensitive geometry with no inference-time overhead. What training removed,
training can be made to put back.

\paragraph{Hallucination detection: a detector from a first principle
(\Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}).}
The third study shows that hallucination in large language models is
\emph{detectable} from the representation the model itself computes. The
information-theoretic argument is constructive. Cross-layer mutual information in the
residual stream is positive by the data-processing structure of the transformer, and
an InfoNCE objective cannot descend below its information floor unless that signal
exists. The probe's trainability is therefore the evidence that the signal is
present. On this principle we build a one-class contrastive probe over cross-layer
activation pairs (\Cref{sec:hd:method}). It \emph{matches-or-outperforms, in the
mean,} the strongest engineered probe (ACT-ViT;~\citealp{barshalom2025actvit})
across the evaluated models and datasets (\Cref{sec:hd:results}). This is not a
leaderboard victory. The method reaches \emph{parity} with the best learned baseline
rather than dominating it, and the parity is the point: a detector derived from a
first principle that lands in the same performance band as a heavily engineered one
is evidence that the principle is right, because a wrong account of where the signal
lives would not land there. The study also makes a \emph{falsifiable} prediction and
confirms it. The theory says that symmetric supervision should fail when one class
lacks coherent latent structure (\Cref{cor:hd:symcon}), and the corresponding
ablation degrades as predicted.

\subsection{Synthesis: Three Studies, One Thesis}
\label{subsec:concl:synthesis}

On their own, the three studies address different tasks, different data, and
different model families. What makes them one dissertation rather than three papers
is that each asks the same question, \emph{does the representation preserve what
distinguishes the shift?}, at a different point along one arc.

The arc, \emph{failure, recovery, detection}, is not just a narrative order. It is a
progression in what the representation makes available. \Cref{ch:label-blindness}
marks the boundary of the possible: the distinguishing structure is \emph{absent}
from the representation by construction, so detection fails not for want of a better
score but for want of any signal to score. \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse}
sits just inside that boundary: the structure was available in the data, but
training \emph{destroyed} it, and we show both why this happens and how to put it
back. \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection} works where the structure is
\emph{retained}: the model keeps the distinction in its intermediate activations,
and the task is to read it out. The three cases are structure absent, suppressed,
and retained, and the detectability of the shift tracks how much structure survives.

The two lenses are what let the same question be asked in such different settings.
Label blindness and hallucination detection are stated in the language of
information theory: the label-relevant signal is mutual information, and detection is
feasible only when that mutual information is non-zero, a condition the
data-processing inequality (\Cref{def:bg:dpi}) makes precise in both.
Domain-sensitivity collapse is stated in the language of geometry: the signal is
variance along domain-shift directions, and detection degrades when those directions
collapse into the class subspace (\Cref{def:bg:clssubspace}). The two descriptions
are not rivals. The information a representation discards is the same thing as the
directions it flattens. Reading each study through whichever lens is sharper is what
reveals the common condition beneath three failures that look unrelated. That one
condition, read through two lenses, governs unlabeled OOD detection, single-domain
OOD detection, and LLM hallucination detection alike is the central contribution of
this work.

\section{Broader Implications}
\label{sec:concl:implications}

The contributions above have implications beyond their immediate technical settings,
for how reliable machine learning is built, analyzed, and evaluated.

\subsection{AI Safety and Reliability}
\label{subsec:concl:safety}

Most work on the reliability of deployed models is empirical: a detector is
proposed, measured on a benchmark, and adopted if it scores well. This dissertation
argues for a second, foundational discipline that asks \emph{when} a class of
methods can work at all, before asking how well a particular method performs.
Knowing that a label-independent detector is guaranteed to fail in the overlap
regime (\Cref{ch:label-blindness}) is itself a safety contribution. It tells a
practitioner not to trust unlabeled detection where in- and out-of-distribution data
share features, however well it scored on a non-overlapping benchmark. The same
posture runs through the other two studies. Diagnosing domain-sensitivity collapse
tells practitioners who deploy narrow-domain models, in medical imaging, remote
sensing, or industrial inspection, why their distance- and logit-based monitors may
be quietly insensitive, and it gives them a training-time remedy that costs nothing
at inference. Locating the hallucination signal in a model's own activations gives
factuality-critical applications a detection route that needs no sampling overhead.
In each case the safety value comes from a principled account of the failure mode,
not from an incremental benchmark gain. A reliability mechanism whose guarantees are
understood is more trustworthy than one validated only by the benchmarks it happens
to have been tested on~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple}.

\subsection{Information Theory and Representation Geometry in Machine Learning}
\label{subsec:concl:lenses}

The methodological lesson of this work is that ``what does the representation
preserve?'' is a portable and productive question, and that it can be asked in two
languages. Information theory supplied the impossibility result and the feasibility
argument. Geometry supplied the diagnosis and the recovery. The synthesis above
shows the two describing the same quantity. The data-processing inequality, the
variational mutual-information bounds, and the geometric notions of effective rank
and subspace collapse are not special-purpose tools for these three problems. They
are general instruments for reasoning about reliability, and this dissertation is in
part a demonstration of their reach. The neural-collapse geometry that explains
single-domain failure (\Cref{def:bg:neuralcollapse}) and the cross-layer mutual
information that supports hallucination detection are, at bottom, the same kind of
statement about preserved structure, and treating them as such is what unifies the
work.

\subsection{Evaluation and Benchmarking}
\label{subsec:concl:eval}

A recurring theme is that benchmarks should be built to expose predicted failure
modes, not merely to be convenient. The Adjacent OOD benchmark
(\Cref{ch:label-blindness}) exists because standard near/far benchmarks avoid the
overlap regime in which label blindness appears, and so report optimistic numbers
for methods that the theory predicts will fail in deployment. The single-domain
evaluation behind domain-sensitivity collapse addresses a similar gap, between
laboratory practice, which favors multi-domain settings, and real deployment, which
is often narrow-domain. The broader point is that evaluation design is not neutral.
A benchmark that cannot express a predicted failure cannot catch it, and confidence
in a method is only as sound as the regimes its benchmarks actually probe.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl:limitations}

The honest scope of this dissertation is narrower than its ambitions, and stating
the limits plainly is part of defending the claims. The thesis is that the work
establishes \emph{when} detection is possible and \emph{that} principled methods
reach that limit. It does not claim that the methods are optimal, or that the
conditions hold without idealization.

\paragraph{Label blindness.}
The guaranteed-failure result rests on an idealization: \emph{strict} statistical
independence between the surrogate objective and the label-relevant features. Real
self-supervised objectives are rarely perfectly independent of the label
distinction. They are partially aligned, so the theorem bounds a worst case rather
than predicting exact field performance. The Adjacent OOD benchmark builds one
regime of high feature overlap. It is a sharp instrument for the predicted failure,
but it is one constructed regime, not the whole space of shifts a deployed system
may face.

\paragraph{Domain-sensitivity collapse.}
The bounds of \Cref{ch:domain-sensitivity-collapse} are diagnostic and must not be
read as guarantees. They explain and predict where scorers lose sensitivity, but
they do not prove that recovery is impossible without intervention, and TGT in fact
recovers it. The recovery method inherits the coverage of its teacher. TGT distills
a frozen multi-domain teacher, so the geometry it can restore is bounded by the
domains that teacher represents. The empirical validation spans several
single-domain benchmarks, but it is not exhaustive across modalities or application
areas.

\paragraph{Hallucination detection.}
The hallucination detector reaches \emph{parity} with the strongest engineered
baseline rather than surpassing it, and the parity is uneven. The method wins
clearly on some dataset-model cells, ties on others, and loses firmly on the Natural
Questions cells, which we report rather than hide (\Cref{sec:hd:results}). The
feasibility-by-trainability argument shows that the label-relevant signal
\emph{exists} in the cross-layer representation. It does not show that this
particular probe extracts it optimally, and a better readout may exist. The
evaluation covers two open-weights model families at the $8$B scale and a fixed set
of question-answering datasets (\Cref{sec:hd:limitations}). Transfer to larger
models, to closed models, and to other task formats is bounded by what we tested.

\paragraph{The thesis, scoped honestly.}
The three studies share one limitation, worth stating once. The dissertation maps
the limit of what is detectable and shows that principled methods reach it. It does
not claim that the methods are the best possible at that limit, or that the
idealized conditions hold exactly in every deployment. Keeping that distinction
clear is what keeps the limitations from undercutting the spine: the contribution is
the \emph{condition}, and the demonstration that it can be met, not a claim of
optimality.

\section{Future Directions}
\label{sec:concl:future}

The limitations above are not dead ends. Each points to a concrete open problem. We
highlight three that follow directly from the work and that this dissertation does
not answer.

\paragraph{Testing for label blindness \emph{a priori}.}
The label blindness theorem currently works as a diagnosis: given a surrogate
objective, one can reason about whether it is independent of the label distinction.
A more useful tool would \emph{predict} blindness before training. It would be an
estimable quantity, computed from an objective and a label set, that signals whether
a self-supervised method is at risk of label blindness for a given deployment. Such
a test would turn the worst-case theorem into a practical screening procedure, and
it would address the idealization noted above by measuring \emph{how} independent a
real objective is rather than assuming strict independence.

\paragraph{An information-theoretic dual for domain-sensitivity collapse.}
Domain-sensitivity collapse was developed deliberately in geometric terms, and the
synthesis of \Cref{subsec:concl:synthesis} argues that its geometric statement and
an information-theoretic one should be equivalent. Making that equivalence formal, by
deriving the variance-suppression account of DSC from a mutual-information argument
or the reverse, would do more than tidy the theory. It would test the dissertation's
central claim that the two lenses describe one quantity, on the one study where only
a single lens has so far been applied.

\paragraph{From detection to mitigation, and across model families.}
The cross-layer probe detects hallucination from a model's activations. A natural
next step is to ask whether the same signal can be used to \emph{steer} generation
away from hallucination, which would turn a detector into a mitigation mechanism.
Transfer is equally open: whether the construction holds across model families and
at scales beyond those evaluated here, including larger and closed models. Both
questions follow from the scope limits of \Cref{ch:hallucination-detection}, and
both would test whether the feasibility-by-trainability principle generalizes as
cleanly as the theory suggests.

\section{Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:concl:remarks}

The reliability of a machine learning system in the open world is, at bottom, a
question about its representation: does it still carry the structure that would let
it recognize when something has changed? This dissertation has argued that one
condition, whether the representation preserves the distinguishing structure,
governs reliability across settings as different as unlabeled out-of-distribution
detection, single-domain detection, and large language model hallucination, and that
information theory and geometry are two ways of asking whether that condition holds.
Along one arc from failure to recovery to detection, we proved where the structure
cannot be present, restored it where training had removed it, and read it out where
the model kept it. The methods are not the last word, and the conditions that draw
the limit are idealizations of a messier reality. But the limit is real, and it is
the same in every case. A model that is to be trusted beyond its training must
preserve what distinguishes the world it will meet from the world it was shown.
