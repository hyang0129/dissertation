\begin{abstract}

\linespread{1.12}\selectfont
Machine learning systems deployed in the open world must recognize when an input,
or an output, falls outside what they can be trusted to handle. Out-of-distribution
(OOD) detection and hallucination detection in large language models are usually
studied apart. This dissertation argues that one condition governs both: detection
is possible exactly when the learned representation preserves the structure that
distinguishes the shift. When the representation does not contain that structure, no
scoring rule can recover it. When training has suppressed it, it can be restored.
When the representation keeps it, a detector can read it out.

We study this condition with two tools. Information theory measures preserved
structure as mutual information, and representation geometry measures it as structure
in feature space; the two describe the same quantity. Three studies apply them along
one arc, from failure to recovery to detection. First, we prove that unlabeled OOD
detection is guaranteed to fail when the self- or unsupervised objective is
independent of the label-relevant features, and we introduce the Adjacent OOD
benchmark, which builds the feature-overlap regime that exposes this failure.
Second, we identify domain-sensitivity collapse, the geometric mechanism by which
single-domain supervised training suppresses the directions along which a shift
would register, and we present Teacher-Guided Training, which restores
domain-sensitive geometry with no inference-time overhead. Third, we derive a
hallucination detector from an information-theoretic feasibility argument and show
that it matches or, in the mean, outperforms the strongest engineered probe.
Reaching that parity is itself evidence that the underlying principle is correct.

The main contribution is the unifying representation-structure framework. The
dissertation shows both when detection is possible and that principled methods
reach that limit, rather than claiming they are optimal.
\end{abstract}
