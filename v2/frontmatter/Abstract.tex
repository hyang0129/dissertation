\begin{abstract}
% Tighten body leading slightly so the abstract fits on one page (the global

\linespread{1.12}\selectfont
A model deployed in the open world meets inputs its training never covered and is
asked for outputs it has no grounds to give. To be trusted, it has to flag those
cases. Two literatures study that flagging apart: out-of-distribution (OOD)
detection for inputs, hallucination detection for the generations of large language
models. This dissertation shows they answer to a single condition. Detection
succeeds only when the model's learned representation has kept the structure that
distinguishes it. Where that structure was never encoded, a detector has
nothing to score. Where training encoded it and then drove it out, it can be
restored. When the representation keeps it, a detector can read it out.

The condition becomes usable through two measurements. Mutual information quantifies
how much of the relevant distinction a representation has retained, and feature-space
geometry exposes the same loss as variance that has thinned along the very axes a
shift would perturb. The two track one quantity, and each study took up whichever
read more cleanly. The first proves that label-free detection must fail once the
self- or unsupervised objective shares nothing with the features that fix the label,
and it contributes the Adjacent OOD benchmark to construct the feature overlap that
brings the failure out. The second identifies domain-sensitivity collapse: training
on a single domain folds the representation into a class-aligned subspace and
suppresses the shift directions, a loss that Teacher-Guided Training reverses by
distilling a frozen multi-domain teacher, at no inference cost. The third derives a
hallucination detector from a feasibility argument: a contrastive probe over
cross-layer activations matches or, in the mean, outperforms the strongest
engineered probe, and that parity is the evidence its principle is right.

The unifying representation-structure framework is the central contribution. The
dissertation establishes the limit of what detection can reach and shows principled
methods arriving there; it does not claim they are optimal.
\end{abstract}
