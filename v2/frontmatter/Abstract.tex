\begin{abstract}
Machine learning systems deployed in the open world must recognize when an input,
or an output, falls outside what they can be trusted to handle. Out-of-distribution
(OOD) detection and the detection of hallucination in large language models are
usually studied apart, but this dissertation argues that they are two faces of one
question and are governed by one condition: detection is possible exactly when the
learned representation preserves the structure that distinguishes the shift. Where
that structure is absent from the representation, no scoring rule can recover it;
where training has suppressed it, it can be restored; and where the representation
retains it, a principled detector can read it out.

We examine this condition through two interchangeable lenses---information theory,
which measures preserved structure as mutual information, and representation
geometry, which measures it as structure in feature space---across three studies
that trace a single arc from failure to recovery to detection. First, we prove that
unlabeled OOD detection is guaranteed to fail when the self- or unsupervised
objective is independent of the label-relevant features, and we introduce the
Adjacent OOD benchmark, which constructs the feature-overlap regime that exposes
this failure. Second, we identify domain-sensitivity collapse, the geometric
mechanism by which single-domain supervised training suppresses the directions along
which a shift would register, and we present Teacher-Guided Training, which restores
domain-sensitive geometry with no inference-time overhead. Third, we derive a
hallucination detector from an information-theoretic feasibility argument and show
that it matches or, in the mean, outperforms the strongest engineered probe---a
parity that is itself evidence the underlying principle is correct.

The central contribution is the unifying representation-structure framework and the
demonstration that principled methods reach the frontier of detectability: the
dissertation establishes when detection is possible, and that such methods attain
it, rather than claiming optimality.
\end{abstract}
